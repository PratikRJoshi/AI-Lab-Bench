## Analysis

### Claim Validation

**Claim 1 — "You are not chasing a person, you are chasing a feeling: validation, acceptance, reciprocation."**
*Supported (with nuance).* This is consistent with mainstream psychology: contingent self-worth, need-to-belong (Baumeister & Leary 1995), and attachment-anxiety frameworks all describe romantic preoccupation as partly driven by the prospect of having unmet relational needs answered. It is a useful reframe, but it overgeneralizes — genuine attraction to the specific person, shared values, and companionship also matter. Treating *every* case of pining as pure feeling-chasing is a simplification.

**Claim 2 — "These behaviours are rooted in dopaminergic reward seeking just like chasing a drug."**
*Contested / partly misleading.* The underlying neuroscience — that the mesolimbic dopamine system encodes reward prediction and motivational salience ("wanting" in Berridge's terminology, "reward prediction error" in Schultz's) — is real, and romantic pursuit/early-stage love does activate dopaminergic pathways (Fisher et al., Aron et al., fMRI studies). However, the "just like chasing a drug" equivalence is the classic pop-neuroscience overreach: drugs of abuse (cocaine, amphetamines, opioids) produce *supraphysiological* dopamine release and direct pharmacological hijacking of the reward system, whereas social/romantic reward operates within normal physiological ranges and is shaped by many other neuromodulators (oxytocin, vasopressin, serotonin, endogenous opioids). The analogy is rhetorically vivid but mechanistically loose; addiction researchers (e.g., Volkow, Heyman) caution against flattening every motivated behaviour into "dopamine = addiction."

**Claim 3 — "You don't want them, you want them to want you."**
*Partially supported.* For anxiously attached or validation-seeking individuals, this captures a real pattern documented in attachment and self-esteem literature (Crocker & Wolfe on contingent self-worth, Mikulincer & Shaver on attachment anxiety). For securely attached people in mutual relationships, it is empirically false. Presented as a universal truth, the claim is a category error.

**Claim 4 (implied) — "Channel that available dopamine back into yourself."**
*Mechanistically vague; behaviourally reasonable.* Dopamine is not a fluid you can pour into a different bucket; you cannot consciously "redirect" tonic or phasic dopamine signaling. What the advice *operationally* means — redirect attention and effortful behaviour toward self-investment, novel rewarding activities, and goal pursuit — is genuinely supported by behavioural-activation therapy and the broader CBT literature for low mood and rumination. The science is the metaphor's wrapper; the wrapper is wrong, the contents are mostly fine.

## Verdict
The post is a well-packaged piece of psychological self-help dressed up in shaky neuroscience. The core *psychological* reframe — "you're chasing the feeling, not the person, so invest in yourself" — is broadly supported by attachment, self-worth, and behavioural-activation research, and the practical takeaway (redirect attention and effort onto yourself) is a reasonable nudge. The *neuroscientific* claim that this is mechanistically equivalent to chasing a drug is the standard pop-dopamine oversimplification: directionally true that the same brain systems are involved, but the "just like a drug" equivalence misrepresents the magnitude and pharmacology of addiction. Useful as a 15-second mood-shift; not useful as a model of the brain. Don't expect 15 seconds of reframing to produce durable change.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
The advice — stop fixating on them, put that energy into yourself — is solid and is basically what a therapist would tell you. The neuroscience bit ("it's just like a drug addiction in your brain") is the part to take with salt: real drug addiction hijacks your brain way harder than a crush does, and you can't literally redirect dopamine like changing a Wi-Fi password. Treat it as a good vibe-shift reminder, not a brain manual. Thumbs up for the message, gentle eye-roll at the neuro-speak.
