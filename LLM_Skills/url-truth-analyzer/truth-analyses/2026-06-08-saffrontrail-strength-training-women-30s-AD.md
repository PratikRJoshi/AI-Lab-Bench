## Analysis

### Science / EBM
The general claims are well-supported:
- **Muscle loss starts in the 30s; strength training slows/reverses it**: age-related muscle loss begins around the 30s and accelerates later (esp. post-menopause); resistance training is the established intervention to slow and partly reverse it [1][2]. ✔
- **Muscle as a "metabolic engine" — blood sugar, hormones, joint health, longevity**: skeletal muscle is a major site of glucose disposal and is linked to metabolic health, functional independence, and lower mortality — an accurate, if punchy, framing [1][2]. ✔
- **Protein non-negotiable for muscle repair/building**: adequate protein (~0.8–1.2 g/kg/day or higher for trainers) is required to translate training into muscle; this is standard sports-nutrition guidance [1]. ✔
- **Independence/mobility later in life**: loss of muscle drives falls, difficulty with stairs/carrying, and disability — accurate [1][2].

The **product-specific** claims are marketing and not independently verified here: "27 g clean whey isolate," "99% lactose-free," and especially "clinically tested enzymes that improve protein absorption" — the absorption-enhancing-enzyme claim is a brand marketing assertion with weak/contested independent evidence. Whole-food protein achieves the same goal; whey is a convenience, not a necessity.

## Verdict
The educational claims (C1–C3) are **supported** at strong grade — starting strength training in your 30s, muscle as a metabolic/longevity asset, and protein being essential are all accurate and important. **But this is a disclosed paid advertisement** for MuscleBlaze Biozyme whey, and its product-specific selling points — especially "enzymes that improve protein absorption" (C4) — are weakly-evidenced marketing. Whole-food protein works just as well. Share the strength-training message; treat the whey pitch as the ad it's labelled to be.
