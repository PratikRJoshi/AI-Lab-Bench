# Truth Analysis: Science Vs — "Tattoos and cancer: relative vs absolute risk"

**Bottom line**: Excellent, accurate science communication — the studies showing ~21% higher lymphoma and ~30% higher melanoma risk in tattooed people are real, but these are *relative* risks on a low baseline, so any individual's absolute risk rises only slightly. "Take seriously, don't freak out" is the right read.

## Summary
Science journalist Rose Rimler addresses a scary stat ("21% chance of malignant lymphoma with even small tattoos"). She clarifies: there are studies finding increased cancer risk in tattooed people — one found ~20% higher lymphoma risk and the same researchers ~30% higher melanoma risk — but these are *relative risks* at the population level. For an average person, baseline lymphoma risk (~2.2%) would rise to ~2.64%, and melanoma to ~2.9%. So it's "something to take seriously, but not to freak out about."

## Analysis

### Science / EBM
The numbers and — crucially — the framing are accurate. The Lund University population-based case–control study (Nielsen et al., 2024; 11,905 people) found tattooed individuals had a 21% higher adjusted risk of overall lymphoma (IRR 1.21; 95% CI 0.99–1.48), strongest for diffuse large B-cell and follicular lymphoma [1]. The same group's 2025 melanoma study found a 29% increased relative risk (rising to ~32% when excluding people on immunosuppressants) [2]. Both papers explicitly state these are **group-level relative risks**, causality cannot be inferred from a single epidemiological study, and results need replication [1][2] — exactly Rimler's point. Her conversion to absolute risk (≈2.2%→2.64% lymphoma; →~2.9% melanoma) correctly demonstrates that a "21–30% increase" does **not** mean a 21–30% chance for an individual. This is textbook-correct risk communication. (Minor note: the lymphoma confidence interval crosses 1.0, so even the relative association is suggestive rather than definitive — which only reinforces her "don't freak out" conclusion.)

## Verdict
All three claims **supported** — the studies are real (C1) [1][2], the relative-vs-absolute distinction is correctly applied (C2), and "take it seriously without panicking" is the right interpretation given suggestive, non-causal associations (C3). This is exactly how risk stats should be communicated. Definitely worth sharing. Citations verified directly.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
Yes, tattooed people show a bit more lymphoma and melanoma in studies — but "21% higher" is the scary-sounding *relative* number. In real terms your odds go from about 2.2% to 2.6%, not "21% chance you'll get cancer." Worth knowing, not worth panicking over.
