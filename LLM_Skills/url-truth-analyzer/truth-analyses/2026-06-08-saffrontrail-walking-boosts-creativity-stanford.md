## Analysis

### Science
The cited study is **Oppezzo & Schwartz (2014), "Give Your Ideas Some Legs"** (*J. Experimental Psychology: LMC*), exactly as she describes [1][2]:
- **~60% boost**: "the average increase in creative output was around 60%" when walking vs. sitting [1][2]. ✔
- **Treadmill / indoor / outdoor**: tested on treadmill indoors and walking outdoors; walking itself (not the environment) was the driver [1]. ✔
- **Residual effect**: when seated *after* walking, participants showed a "residual creative boost" [2]. ✔ matches "you continue to carry that state with you."
- **Specific to divergent thinking**: walking improved Guilford's Alternate Uses (divergent/idea-generation) but *mildly hurt* the convergent (single-answer) task [2]. She frames it as general "creative thinking," which is essentially right but technically narrower — it's divergent ideation, not focused problem-solving.
- A **2024 meta-analysis** (23 studies, ~1,036 participants) confirms a large effect on divergent thinking (d≈0.93) and a null effect on convergent thinking [3] — strengthening the claim beyond the single study.

The historical anecdotes (Darwin, Beethoven, Jobs) are well-documented and used appropriately as illustration, not evidence.

## Verdict
Claims **supported** at strong grade. The ~60% figure, the carry-over effect, and the indoor/outdoor finding all come straight from Oppezzo & Schwartz (2014) [1][2], and a 2024 meta-analysis backs the broader pattern [3]. The only refinement: the boost is specifically to *divergent* (idea-generating) thinking, not focused single-answer problem-solving — but as everyday advice ("go walk to get unstuck"), it's accurate and genuinely useful. Worth sharing.
