## Summary
A short explainer video about why Cadbury changed the shape of the Dairy Milk bar from angular to rounder corners (~2013 in the UK), and why consumers perceived it as sweeter despite no recipe change. The creator attributes this to "gastrophysics" — specifically, that rounder shapes stimulate a sweeter taste perception on the tongue than sharp/angular shapes. The video cites two reasons for the shape change: economics (smaller volume for same dimensions = less chocolate = reduced cost) and a secondary effect of triggering sweetness perception.

## Analysis

### Claim Validation

**Claim 1: Cadbury changed the Dairy Milk shape around 2013 in the UK, making it more rounded, and consumers complained it was sweeter despite no recipe change.**
- **Status**: ✅ Supported
- This event is well-documented. In 2013, Cadbury (then owned by Kraft/Mondeléz) redesigned the Dairy Milk bar to have rounder, domed segments. There was a widely covered public backlash in the UK. Cadbury publicly confirmed the recipe had not changed.
- Sources: [BBC News – Cadbury shape change backlash (2013)](https://www.bbc.com/news/uk-england-birmingham-22670537), [The Guardian coverage]

**Claim 2: For the same dimensions, curved/rounder shapes have smaller volumes than angular shapes, meaning less chocolate per bar.**
- **Status**: ✅ Supported (basic geometry)
- A rectangular prism has larger volume than a domed/rounded equivalent with the same bounding dimensions. This is a straightforward geometric fact. It was a widely reported explanation for the redesign — cost savings via volume reduction.

**Claim 3: Rounder shapes taste sweeter on the tongue than straight/angular shapes — this is "gastrophysics."**
- **Status**: ✅ Supported — well-established in experimental food psychology
- This is a real and well-replicated phenomenon in sensory science. It is part of the broader field of "crossmodal correspondence" studied extensively by Prof. Charles Spence (University of Oxford) and covered in his book *Gastrophysics* (2017).
- The phenomenon is specifically called **shape–taste correspondence**: angular, pointed shapes are associated with bitter/sour/salty tastes, while round shapes are associated with sweet tastes.
- This has been replicated across multiple studies including blind taste tests.

**Claim 4: You can use rounder dessert shapes to reduce added sugar while maintaining sweetness perception.**
- **Status**: ✅ Plausible and scientifically grounded
- Follows directly from Claim 3. Reducing sugar while maintaining perceived sweetness through shape manipulation is a legitimate food design strategy. It has not been tested at scale in commercial products beyond anecdotal evidence, but the underlying mechanism is valid.
