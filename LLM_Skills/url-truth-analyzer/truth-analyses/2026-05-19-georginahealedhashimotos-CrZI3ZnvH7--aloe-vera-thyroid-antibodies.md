### EBM SORT Analysis

**Extracted claim** (reconstructed from thumbnail + prop, since no spoken content):
> *"Aloe vera (juice / fresh leaf) can reduce thyroid antibodies in Hashimoto's thyroiditis."*

#### Pass 1 — Four lenses

- **Safety**: Oral aloe vera juice (decolorized / inner-leaf gel) at culinary-to-supplement doses (~50 mL/day, the dose used in the only supportive study) is generally tolerated short-term, but whole-leaf preparations contain anthraquinones (aloin) that are laxative and have a NTP rodent carcinogenicity signal at high chronic doses; aloe latex is on the FDA's "not generally recognized as safe" list for OTC laxative use (2002 ruling). Oral aloe can also interact with diuretics, digoxin (potassium loss), and oral hypoglycemics, and case reports describe aloe-associated hepatitis. The reel does not specify decolorized vs. whole-leaf, dose, or duration — a safety-relevant omission.
- **Outcomes (POEM vs DOE)**: Every outcome the supporting study measured (TPO antibody titer, TSH, free T4) is a **surrogate/disease-oriented (DOE)** marker. The patient-oriented outcomes that matter — sustained drug-free euthyroidism, prevention of overt hypothyroidism progression, normal fertility/pregnancy outcomes, prevention of long-term cardiovascular/cognitive sequelae — were not measured. A 56% drop in TPO over 9 months is biologically interesting but does not equal "cured Hashimoto's."
- **Risk of bias**: The Metro 2018 study (n=30 treatment, n=15 control) is non-randomized, non-blinded, has no placebo, no allocation concealment, single-center, single research group, no pre-registration. Patient self-selection between treatment and control is likely. Regression to the mean is unaddressed despite high baseline TPO (>400 U/mL — a known regressor). For a chronic autoimmune disease with naturally waxing/waning antibody titers, an open-label design over 9 months is particularly prone to overestimating effect size.
- **Total evidence**: One study, n=45 total, 2018, with **no independent replication in 8 years**. PubMed and Cochrane return no follow-up trial, no meta-analysis including aloe vera in Hashimoto's, and no major endocrine guideline (ATA 2014/2024 update, Endocrine Society, NICE NG145, ETA) mentions aloe vera as an intervention. Compare with selenium, which has 10+ RCTs, multiple meta-analyses (e.g., Wang 2024, PMID 39698034), and is still only positioned as adjunct — not replacement — therapy.

#### Pass 2 — SORT grade

**Grade C** for the claim "aloe vera reduces thyroid antibodies in Hashimoto's."

Justification: Recommendation based on **limited-quality or inconsistent patient-oriented evidence, or on disease-oriented evidence only**. Specifically: (a) outcomes measured are DOE, not POEM; (b) only one supporting study exists; (c) that study has significant methodological limitations (no blinding, no placebo, non-randomized); (d) no replication in nearly a decade; (e) no inclusion in any major endocrine guideline. The honest framing is "intriguing preliminary signal that needs an actual RCT," not "this reduces thyroid antibodies."

#### What the reel does NOT say but should

- The supporting study used subclinical hypothyroidism, not overt — patients with overt hypothyroidism on levothyroxine should not interpret this as a license to stop their medication.
- The supporting study used a specific 50 mL/day juice preparation for 9 months — not a single fresh leaf eaten on camera. The prop in the reel is theatrical, not the studied intervention.
- Whole-leaf aloe (which includes the latex/aloin) is *not* the same product as decolorized inner-leaf juice and carries different safety considerations.
- TPO antibody reduction ≠ Hashimoto's cure. Many people have positive TPO antibodies for life without becoming hypothyroid, and many become hypothyroid even when antibodies fall.

## Verdict
The hook on the thumbnail — *"WANNA REDUCE THYROID ANTIBODIES?"* paired with aloe vera as the visual answer — is not fabricated, but it is materially oversold. It rests on one open-label, non-randomized, non-blinded, non-placebo-controlled study of 30 women in 2018 that has not been independently replicated, measures surrogate (DOE) endpoints rather than patient-oriented (POEM) outcomes, and is absent from every major endocrinology guideline. That's SORT Grade C. The 3.6-second silent reel doesn't disclose the dose, preparation, duration, or single-study basis, and the visual prop (a fresh whole leaf with a grocery-store price tag) is not the studied intervention (50 mL/day of a specific juice preparation). Combined with the channel's well-documented incentive to market a "heal Hashimoto's naturally" program, this should be treated as a marketing hook on top of a thin, unreplicated signal — interesting enough to mention to a clinician, nowhere near strong enough to act on or substitute for levothyroxine.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
There is actually one real study from 2018 where 30 women drank aloe vera juice for 9 months and their thyroid antibody numbers dropped a lot — so the post isn't pulled out of thin air. But it's *one* study, not blinded, no placebo, never repeated since, and nobody's endocrinology guideline mentions aloe. Lower antibody numbers also don't mean "cured." Eating a whole grocery-store aloe leaf on camera is also not the same thing as the studied juice. So: an interesting hint, not a treatment — and don't drop your thyroid meds over it.
