## Summary

Dietitian Soumya claims that for every 1 gram of protein consumed, the body requires approximately **10 ml of water** and **0.35 g of fiber** to support healthy metabolism, kidney function, and gut microbiome balance. She frames this not as a universal rule but as a "functional system" — a practical heuristic. The reasoning given: protein digestion generates nitrogen load and urea that requires water for renal excretion (osmotic load), and fiber is needed to modulate what gut bacteria do with unabsorbed protein in the colon (the "microbiome loop"). The tagline: "Everyone is increasing protein. Very few are supporting it."

## Analysis

### EBM SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Lenses

**1. Safety lens**

No harms from the recommendation are discussed, which is appropriate — increasing water and fiber intake alongside protein is generally safe. However, no mention of populations for whom this ratio may be inappropriate:
- **Chronic kidney disease (CKD)** patients: increased protein *and* increased fluid intake may worsen their condition depending on stage. High-protein diets are contraindicated or restricted in CKD stages 3–5.
- **Fluid-restricted patients** (heart failure, SIADH, late-stage CKD): a rule encouraging "10ml water per gram of protein" could be harmful.
- **High-fiber caution**: Rapidly increasing fiber without adequate water (the very pairing she recommends) can cause bloating and constipation paradoxically if water intake doesn't keep pace.

The omission of these contraindications is a meaningful gap for a health professional's public-facing content, though the post is clearly aimed at healthy general public.

**2. Outcomes lens**

| Claim | Type |
|---|---|
| Protein generates osmotic load requiring water for renal clearance | DOE (mechanistic physiology) |
| Fiber modulates gut microbiome response to unabsorbed protein | DOE (microbiome marker) |
| 10ml water per gram protein ratio | DOE (derived heuristic, no patient outcome data) |
| 0.35g fiber per gram protein ratio | DOE (derived heuristic, no patient outcome data) |

All claims are disease-oriented or mechanistic. No patient-oriented outcomes (reduced kidney stones, better kidney function measures, reduced constipation rates, improved nitrogen balance in trials) are cited.

**3. Risk of bias lens**

- The **10ml water / 1g protein** ratio: This is a *derived heuristic*, not a number from a clinical trial. The physiological basis is sound in principle — protein catabolism produces urea and nitrogen waste that requires renal water for excretion. The urine osmolality of protein metabolites does create a measurable water demand. However, the specific "10ml per gram" figure does not appear in standard clinical nutrition or nephrology guidelines. It is a reasonable back-of-envelope estimate, not an evidence-based standard.
- The **0.35g fiber / 1g protein** ratio: Similarly, this is not a figure from any nutrition guideline or clinical trial. The mechanism cited (fiber controlling gut bacterial protein fermentation / putrefaction) is real — colonic protein fermentation produces potentially harmful byproducts (ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, phenols) that fiber can help modulate by shifting fermentation toward carbohydrates. However, the specific 0.35g figure is not sourced and does not appear in the literature.
- No citations are provided for either ratio.
- The author is identified as a dietitian (Soumya Reddy), lending professional credibility, but the specific numerical claims are self-derived or from an undisclosed source.

**4. Total evidence lens**

The *directional* claims are well-supported:

1. **Protein increases renal water demand**: True. High-protein diets increase urine osmolality and urine volume requirements. Studies show high-protein diets increase renal solute load. The general advice to increase fluid intake on high-protein diets is consistent with dietetics guidelines (Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, ISSN).

2. **Fiber modulates protein fermentation in the colon**: True. When dietary protein exceeds small intestinal absorption, it passes to the colon where bacteria ferment it, producing ammonia, branched-chain fatty acids, and other metabolites. Adequate fiber shifts bacterial fermentation toward saccharolytic (carbohydrate-fermenting) rather than proteolytic pathways. This is established in gut microbiome research.

3. **The specific numerical ratios (10ml / 0.35g per gram protein)**: **Not established in literature as universal thresholds**. These are plausible heuristics but are presented with a precision the underlying evidence does not support.

The overall dietary advice (drink more water and eat more fiber when eating more protein) is sound and evidence-consistent. The specific numbers are invented heuristics, not evidence-based prescriptions.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade

**Overall: Grade C**

The mechanistic reasoning (nitrogen load, osmotic demand, microbiome modulation) is scientifically grounded. However, the specific numerical ratios (10ml water and 0.35g fiber per gram of protein) are not derived from RCTs, systematic reviews, or established clinical guidelines. They are expert-derived heuristics stated with quantitative precision that implies a level of evidence that does not exist. The general direction of advice (hydrate and fiber up alongside protein) is Grade B–A territory, but the specific numerical claim as stated is Grade C (expert-derived formula, not clinically validated).

## Verdict

The **core message is scientifically grounded**: protein metabolism does create a nitrogen/urea load requiring water for clearance, and fiber does help manage colonic protein fermentation. The general advice to increase water and fiber alongside protein is sound and consistent with nutrition science. However, the **specific numerical ratios — 10ml water and 0.35g fiber per gram of protein — are self-derived heuristics, not established clinical standards**, and are presented with a quantitative precision that the underlying evidence does not support. The author's own caveat ("not universal, but a functional system") partially acknowledges this. For healthy adults eating moderate-to-high protein diets, following this heuristic is unlikely to cause harm and may be beneficial. For people with kidney disease, fluid restrictions, or GI disorders, these numbers should not be applied without clinical guidance. Overall: **directionally trustworthy, numerically unverified** — Grade C on the specific ratio claims, Grade B on the directional advice.
