## Summary
A single-page infographic from Functional Nutrition Alliance (fxnutrition.com) titled "16 Tips to Champion Your Liver." It presents 16 dietary, supplement, and lifestyle tips framed as ways to support liver "detoxification." Tips range from well-evidenced (soluble fiber, choline) to weakly supported (turmeric, beets) to pseudoscientific (castor oil packs, skin brushing as detox, the "11pm–3am liver regenerative cycle"). The overarching frame is that the liver needs exogenous help to detoxify — a concept at odds with mainstream hepatology.

## Channel Reputation
**Source channel / handle**: Functional Nutrition Alliance (FxNA), fxnutrition.com — founded by Andrea Nakayama

FxNA is a functional nutrition education company that trains "Certified Functional Nutrition Counselors" (CFNC). It is accredited by the American Association of Drugless Practitioners (AADP) and the American Association of Wellness Practitioners (AANWP) — alternative credentialing bodies, not mainstream medical or dietetic accreditation (not ACEND, not accredited by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics). FxNA's content is consistently framed around "root cause" and "detoxification" narratives. No independent fact-checking coverage of FxNA was found. The organization sells courses, not supplements, but the content serves as lead generation for paid certifications.

## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Analysis Lenses

**Safety**
- The poster does not recommend stopping any medication or replacing medical treatment, which is a positive.
- However, it omits important caveats: milk thistle can interact with drugs metabolized by CYP3A4/CYP2C9 (statins, anticoagulants, etc.); excessive turmeric/curcumin can cause GI distress; high-dose B-complex can cause neuropathy (B6); castor oil ingestion (not mentioned, but implied by "get oily") is a stimulant laxative.
- The framing that your liver needs help detoxifying could lead people with actual liver disease to self-treat with supplements instead of seeing a hepatologist. No disclaimer about consulting a doctor for liver concerns is visible.
- No absolute risks or NNT/NNH for any recommendation.

**Outcomes**
- Every outcome claimed is disease-oriented (DOE) at best: liver enzyme levels, bile flow, "toxin binding," "removing excess estrogens." Zero patient-oriented outcomes (POEMs) — no data on whether these tips reduce liver disease, hospitalization, mortality, or even symptoms.
- The language consistently implies patient benefit from DOEs: "helps the liver repair itself," "clears toxins from the body," "detoxification process." These are mechanistic promises, not demonstrated clinical outcomes.
- Maximum SORT grade is B regardless of study quality, since all outcomes are DOEs.

**Risk of Bias**
- Source is a for-profit education company selling functional nutrition certifications. Content serves to validate the "detox" framework that underpins their curriculum.
- Zero citations on the poster. No study references, no PubMed links, no indication of evidence quality for any claim.
- Several claims derive from traditional/folk medicine (castor oil packs, TCM "liver clock," skin brushing) presented as fact without acknowledging their origin or evidence gaps.
- No conflict of interest disclosure. FxNA profits from people believing the liver needs specialized nutritional support.

**Total Evidence**
- The evidence varies dramatically across the 16 tips. See claim-by-claim breakdown below.
- The overarching "detox" framework is not supported by mainstream hepatology. The healthy liver detoxifies continuously via Phase I (oxidation, CYP450) and Phase II (conjugation) pathways without needing special diets, supplements, or protocols.
- Several tips (fiber, choline, protein moderation) are reasonable general nutrition advice that happens to benefit the liver incidentally — framing them as "liver detox" is misleading.

#### Pass 2 — Claim-by-Claim Assessment & SORT Grades

| # | Claim | Verdict | Grade | Notes |
|---|-------|---------|-------|-------|
| 1 | Liver regenerative cycle is 11pm–3am; late eating disrupts detox | **Contested** | C | TCM "organ clock" concept. Liver has circadian rhythms, but the specific 11pm–3am window is not validated in Western medicine. General advice to avoid late eating has some metabolic support. |
| 2 | 10–12 hours between dinner and breakfast | **Partially supported** | B | Time-restricted eating shows metabolic benefits (glucose, insulin — DOEs). But evidence is mostly observational or short-term RCTs. |
| 3 | Cruciferous veggies support sulfation pathway / estrogen removal | **Partially supported** | B | Glucosinolates do induce Phase II enzymes (including sulfotransferases). Clinical significance for "removing excess estrogens" is overstated. Good mechanistic evidence, limited POEMs. |
| 4 | Lemon zest d-limonene supports liver detox enzymes | **Weakly supported** | C | D-limonene induces Phase I/II enzymes in animal models. Very limited human data. Eating lemon zest is harmless but unlikely to meaningfully affect liver function. |
| 5 | Soluble fiber binds toxins and removes them | **Supported** | B | Fiber binds bile acids and facilitates fecal excretion — well-established. "Toxic flush" language oversells the mechanism, but the core advice (eat fiber) is sound. |
| 6 | Milk thistle/silymarin supports liver repair | **Supported (DOE)** | B | Best-evidenced claim on the poster. Multiple meta-analyses (2024) show significant ALT/AST reductions. But outcomes are DOEs (enzymes), not POEMs (survival/symptoms). Efficacy varies by condition. |
| 7 | Reduce toxins: organic, filtered water, glass, clean skincare | **Partially supported** | B–C | Organic foods reduce pesticide exposure (DOE). Filtered water removes contaminants (established). Glass vs. plastic for BPA — some evidence. Reasonable precautionary advice, overgeneralized. |
| 8 | Excess protein strains the liver | **Contested** | C | True for existing liver disease (hepatic encephalopathy). For healthy adults, high protein intake does not cause liver damage. Overgeneralized claim. |
| 9 | Greens/bitter herbs support liver | **Weakly supported** | C | Bitter compounds stimulate bile production (choleretic effect — established pharmacology). Clinical significance of eating parsley/arugula for liver health is minimal. Cilantro "chelation" claims are unsupported in humans. |
| 10 | Turmeric/curcumin protects liver, promotes bile flow | **Partially supported** | B | Anti-inflammatory properties confirmed in vitro/animal models. Human liver protection evidence limited by curcumin's poor bioavailability (~1% absorbed). Some RCTs show modest NAFLD benefits. |
| 11 | Beets/betaine helps liver clear toxins | **Partially supported** | B | Betaine supports methylation (SAMe cycle) — established biochemistry. Used clinically as adjunct for NASH. Some RCT evidence for DOEs. |
| 12 | Skin brushing removes toxins (skin as "major detox organ") | **Refuted** | C | No evidence. Dermatologists consistently state skin brushing is exfoliation, not detoxification. The liver and kidneys handle detox — not the skin. |
| 13 | Choline for liver triglyceride transport | **Supported** | A–B | Well-established biochemistry: choline is essential for VLDL synthesis. Choline deficiency causes fatty liver (demonstrated in RCTs). One of the strongest claims on this poster. |
| 14 | Castor oil packs open detoxification pathways | **Refuted** | C | No meaningful evidence. Systematic review found only "preliminary" evidence for minor effects. "Opening detoxification pathways" is not a recognized medical concept. |
| 15 | B vitamins for liver detox of heavy metals/histamines | **Partially supported** | B–C | B vitamins are cofactors in Phase I/II reactions (established biochemistry). But "take B-complex to detox heavy metals" grossly oversimplifies and overpromises. |
| 16 | Chlorophyll/chlorella clears heavy metals | **Weakly supported** | C | Chlorella shows mercury binding in animal models. Human evidence extremely limited. Broad "clears heavy metals" claim not supported. |

#### Overall SORT Grade

**Grade C** — The poster presents a mixture of reasonable dietary advice (fiber, choline, vegetables) alongside pseudoscientific claims (castor oil packs, skin brushing, "11pm–3am liver clock"), all wrapped in an unsupported "liver detox" framework with zero citations. Even the better-supported tips (milk thistle, betaine) are evidenced only at the DOE level (liver enzymes), not POEMs (patient outcomes). No single claim reaches Grade A with patient-oriented evidence.

## Verdict
This poster is a representative example of functional nutrition content: it takes a handful of legitimate dietary recommendations (eat fiber, eat vegetables, get enough choline), embeds them in an unsupported "liver detoxification" framework, mixes in folk-medicine practices presented as fact (castor oil packs, skin brushing, the "liver clock"), cites zero sources, and uses it to establish credibility for a for-profit certification program. A healthy liver detoxifies without help. The best advice on this poster — eat vegetables, eat fiber, moderate protein, avoid fried foods — is standard nutrition guidance that doesn't need the "detox" wrapper. The worst advice — castor oil packs, skin brushing as detox, the 11pm–3am regenerative cycle — is pseudoscientific.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
It's complicated. About a third of these tips are actually decent advice — eat fiber, eat your veggies, get enough choline from eggs. But the poster wraps them in a "liver detox" story that's mostly made up. Your liver doesn't need castor oil packs to "open detoxification pathways" (that's not a thing), skin brushing doesn't remove toxins (dermatologists roll their eyes at this), and the "liver regenerates between 11pm–3am" is traditional Chinese medicine, not proven science. The good tips are just normal healthy eating advice. The bad tips are harmless but useless. The real problem is the framing: it makes you think your liver needs special help to function, which it doesn't — unless you actually have liver disease, in which case see a hepatologist, not a poster.
