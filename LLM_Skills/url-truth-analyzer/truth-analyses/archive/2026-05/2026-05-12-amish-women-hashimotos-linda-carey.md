### SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Analysis Lenses

**Safety**
- The claim that a population is "immune" to an autoimmune disease because of lifestyle choices could lead Hashimoto's patients to abandon levothyroxine (their standard treatment) in favor of unproven lifestyle changes.
- The mention of "22 years on Synthroid" and "heart attack at 68" in the caption implies medication failure, which could discourage patients from taking prescribed thyroid hormone replacement.
- No disclaimers, no medical advice caveats, no mention of consulting an endocrinologist.

**Outcomes**
- Zero patient-oriented outcomes (POEMs). The claim is an assertion about population-level disease prevalence, not a treatment outcome.
- No clinical data, no before/after lab values, no remission rates cited.
- The implied outcome — "live like the Amish and you won't get Hashimoto's" — has never been tested in any study.

**Risk of Bias**
- Text-overlay slideshow format with no citations — impossible to verify any specific claim.
- Romanticization of Amish lifestyle is a well-documented misinformation trope used to sell "natural" health narratives (see Amish America debunking article).
- The personal anecdote ("my mother") creates emotional framing that bypasses critical evaluation.
- No study design, no data, no peer review — this is pure assertion.

**Total Evidence**
- **The central claim is directly refuted by peer-reviewed research.**
- Timmers et al. (2003) conducted a genome-wide scan for autoimmune thyroiditis specifically in Old Order Amish families, identifying genetic linkage on chromosome 5q11.2-q14.3. You cannot conduct a genetics study of a disease in a population that doesn't have it.
- Jaume et al. (1999) performed complex segregation analysis of anti-TPO antibodies in Old Order Amish families, finding evidence of autosomal dominant transmission of thyroid autoimmunity.
- Ward et al. (2012) studied thyroid conditions among 2,543 Old Order Amish in Pennsylvania, finding associations with subclinical hypothyroidism in women.
- Amish America's fact-check article documents that many viral Amish health claims are overgeneralized and false, noting that with ~700 different Amish communities, sweeping health generalizations are unreliable.
- Global Hashimoto's prevalence is ~7.5% (systematic review, 2022) and is driven primarily by genetic susceptibility (accounting for 70-80% of risk) — no lifestyle can fully prevent it in genetically predisposed individuals.

## Verdict
The central claim — "Amish women don't get Hashimoto's" — is false. Peer-reviewed genetics research has specifically studied autoimmune thyroiditis in Old Order Amish families, documenting both the disease and its genetic transmission patterns within this population. The video exploits a common misinformation trope: romanticizing a specific community's lifestyle to imply that "natural living" prevents disease. Hashimoto's thyroiditis is predominantly genetic (70–80% of risk) and occurs across all populations regardless of lifestyle. This content could lead patients to abandon effective treatment in pursuit of unproven lifestyle changes.
