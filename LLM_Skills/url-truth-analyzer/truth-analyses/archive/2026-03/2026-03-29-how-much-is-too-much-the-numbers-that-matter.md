## Summary
A 12-image Instagram carousel from Masala Lab (Krish Ashok) that uses striking numerical comparisons to debunk common food myths and put health claims into quantitative perspective. Topics include blood glucose thresholds, caloric needs, glycemic index of sweeteners, MSG safety, sooji vs maida nutrition, gluten-free diets, frozen food preservatives, alcohol safety, vitamin B12 from plants, and food colour toxicity vs salt. The final slide summarizes actionable advice: eat less, prioritize protein and fibre, build muscle, sleep more, avoid alcohol and clickbait.

## Analysis

### EBM SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Analysis Lenses

##### 1. Safety Lens

The carousel makes several safety-adjacent claims:

- **Alcohol: 0g safe amount** — This is consistent with the 2023 WHO position and the Global Burden of Disease studies, which found no safe level of alcohol consumption. Absolute framing (0g) is appropriate here. No harms are omitted — the message is unambiguous.
- **Blood glucose 5g vs 7g** — This is a simplified representation. Total circulating blood glucose in an adult (~5L blood volume) at a fasting level of ~100 mg/dL is approximately 5g, and at diabetic threshold (~140 mg/dL) is approximately 7g. The simplification is directionally correct and used to illustrate how small the difference is, not to diagnose or treat. No dosage or treatment guidance is given.
- **Fatal dose of salt/nutmeg (20g) vs food colours (900g)** — The LD50 comparison is used to argue food colours are far less toxic than salt. The 20g figure for salt aligns with published lethal dose estimates (0.5–1g/kg body weight). The 900g figure for food colours is less well-sourced — LD50 varies widely by specific dye. The rhetorical point (food colours are not acutely dangerous) is valid, but the specific 900g number is an oversimplification.

##### 2. Outcomes Lens

Most claims in this carousel are **disease-oriented evidence (DOEs)** or nutritional facts, not patient-oriented outcomes:

- Blood glucose levels (DOE — surrogate marker)
- Glycemic index values (DOE — surrogate marker)
- Caloric intake thresholds (DOE — energy balance, not mortality/morbidity data)
- MSG glutamate intake percentages (DOE — biochemistry)
- Sooji vs Maida macronutrient comparison (DOE — nutritional composition)
- B12 from plant sources (DOE — biochemistry)
- Fatal dose comparisons (DOE — toxicology)

The only claim approaching a POEM is **"0g safe amount of alcohol"**, which is derived from meta-analyses of mortality and morbidity outcomes (true POEMs). The summary slide's advice (eat less, more protein/fibre, build muscle, sleep) implicitly references POEM-level evidence but does not cite it.

**Maximum grade for most claims: B** (DOE-only evidence for the majority).

##### 3. Risk of Bias Lens

- **Creator credibility**: Krish Ashok is the author of *Masala Lab: The Science of Indian Cooking*, a well-regarded science communication book. He is not a medical professional but presents nutritional science accurately with appropriate caveats.
- **No study citations**: The carousel cites no specific studies, which is typical for social media infographic format. Numbers presented are generally consistent with established nutritional databases (USDA, IFCT) and published literature.
- **Simplification bias**: The biggest risk is oversimplification. Complex topics (glycemic index, caloric needs, gluten sensitivity) are reduced to single numbers without context about individual variation, activity levels, age, or comorbidities.
- **Selection bias**: The slides cherry-pick dramatic numerical comparisons for rhetorical effect (e.g., 5g vs 7g glucose, 20g vs 900g fatal dose). While directionally correct, this framing can be misleading if viewers extrapolate beyond the intended message.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade Assignment

**Overall SORT Grade: B**

**Rationale**: The carousel presents claims that are largely consistent with established nutritional science and clinical guidelines. However:
- Most evidence presented is disease-oriented (DOEs: GI values, calorie numbers, nutrient comparisons) rather than patient-oriented outcomes
- No specific studies or RCTs are cited
- Some claims oversimplify complex topics (gluten sensitivity prevalence, caloric needs variability)
- The alcohol claim (0g safe) is the strongest, backed by large meta-analyses with POEM outcomes
- The overall framing is science-communicator quality — accurate for general public consumption but lacks the specificity and citations needed for Grade A

### Visual Analysis

**Presentation style**: Clean, minimalist infographic design with consistent branding (Masala Lab logo, Totoro-like mascot). Each slide uses a bold number as the visual anchor with a provocative header statement.

**Rhetorical techniques**:
- **Dramatic numerical contrasts**: 5g vs 7g (glucose), 20g vs 900g (fatal dose), 0g (alcohol, B12, preservatives). These create memorable visual anchors.
- **Provocative headers**: "Don't fool yourself", "Gluten-free is a stupid fad", "Calm Down" — designed to challenge popular anxieties and generate engagement.
- **Simplification for impact**: Complex nutritional science reduced to single numbers. Effective for debunking but risks oversimplification.

**Potential misleading elements**:
- The sooji vs maida comparison (Image 6) could imply they're interchangeable, ignoring that particle size affects glycemic response and cooking properties
- The "2% have trouble digesting gluten" framing dismisses NCGS which is a real (if contested) clinical entity
- Calorie targets (1800/2200 kcal) are population averages that vary significantly by body size, age, and activity level

## Verdict

This Masala Lab carousel is a well-crafted piece of science communication that accurately represents the nutritional science consensus on most topics covered. Krish Ashok's numerical framing effectively counters common food anxieties (MSG, food colours, frozen food, gluten for non-celiacs) with data-backed perspective. The alcohol and B12 claims are particularly well-supported by robust evidence. The main weakness is inherent to the format: single numbers cannot capture individual variation in caloric needs, the nuanced spectrum of gluten sensitivity, or the variability in food colour toxicology. Overall, this is trustworthy general-audience content — viewers should treat the numbers as directional guides rather than personal prescriptions, especially for caloric intake and gluten sensitivity. **SORT Grade B** — consistent with established science but presented as disease-oriented evidence without specific study citations.
