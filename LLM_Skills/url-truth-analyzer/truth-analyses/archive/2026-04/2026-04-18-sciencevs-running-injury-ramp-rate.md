## Analysis

### Claim Validation

**Claim 1: "Ramping up too quickly is the one thing that increased runner injury risk like nothing else"**
**SUPPORTED.** Training load spikes are consistently identified as a primary risk factor for running-related injuries across the sports medicine literature. This is the clinical consensus behind the widely taught "10% rule" and the more recent "Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio" (ACWR) framework.

**Claim 2: "Running more than 5% extra was enough to tip them into injury land"**
**CONTESTED — the specific 5% threshold is not well-established.**

The traditional "10% rule" (don't increase weekly mileage by more than 10%) has been commonly cited since the 1980s but has weak prospective evidence behind it. Research shows:
- A 2008 Dutch study found similar injury rates between runners increasing 10% vs 50% per week (20.8% vs 20.3%), suggesting the exact percentage is not a reliable predictor of weekly totals
- Recent BJSM research suggests the more relevant metric is single-run spikes relative to recent longest runs (>10% longer run = 64% higher injury risk; doubling = 128% higher)
- Science Vs may be citing a specific study using a 5% threshold, which could be valid but is more conservative than most guidelines (the traditional rule is 10%, not 5%)

The directional message — that rapid load increases cause injury — is correct. The specific "5%" figure appears to be an oversimplification for a 93-second promo clip; the full podcast episode likely provides more nuance.

**Claim 3: "Does running damage joints? Could it damage your heart?"**
**Both claims referenced but not answered in this promo clip.** The framing ("Is it risky? Does it wear down your joints? Could it even damage your heart?") is teaser language for the full episode. The established science (from the full episode's topic area):
- Joint damage: Recreational running is NOT associated with increased osteoarthritis risk — observational data suggests runners actually have lower rates of knee OA than non-runners (Alentorn-Geli et al., JOSPT 2017)
- Cardiac risk: "Runner's heart" (cardiac remodeling) is a well-studied adaptation that is generally benign; extreme endurance athletes have modestly elevated risk of atrial fibrillation, but recreational running is overwhelmingly cardioprotective
