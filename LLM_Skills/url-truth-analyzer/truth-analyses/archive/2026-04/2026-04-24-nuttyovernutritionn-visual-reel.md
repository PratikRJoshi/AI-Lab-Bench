## Analysis

### SORT Analysis (EBM Strength of Recommendation Taxonomy)

#### Claims to evaluate
1. This homemade powder is a meaningful "calcium powder."
2. Taking 2 spoons daily for 7 days before your period reduces menstrual pain.
3. It improves energy levels around menstruation.
4. It results in "better flow."

---

#### Pass 1 — Four Analysis Lenses

**1. Safety**
- The individual ingredients are common food items and are generally safe for most people.
- **Major omission**: No mention of allergen warnings — this powder contains tree nuts (walnuts), sesame, and possibly poppy seeds, all of which are common allergens.
- **Dosage is imprecise**: "2 spoons" is not a standardized measure (teaspoon vs. tablespoon). Clinical studies that found calcium beneficial used precisely measured 1,000 mg elemental calcium daily.
- No mention of when to seek medical advice for severe dysmenorrhea, which can indicate endometriosis, fibroids, or other conditions requiring treatment.
- No absolute risk/benefit numbers are provided.

**2. Outcomes**
- "Less pain" is a patient-oriented outcome (POEM) — this is the strongest claim and does have *some* basis, though not for this specific preparation (see below).
- "Better energy" is vague and unmeasurable as stated — neither a clear POEM nor DOE.
- "Better flow" is ambiguous — it's unclear whether this means lighter flow, more regular flow, or something else. No clinical definition is offered.
- **Critical calcium dosage gap**: Whole dried sesame seeds contain ~975 mg calcium per 100g, the richest ingredient here. If the powder is ~25% sesame seeds, then 2 tablespoons (~20g) of the mix would yield ~5g sesame seeds, providing roughly **49 mg of calcium**. The remaining ingredients contribute minimal calcium (makhana: 60 mg/100g, walnuts: 98 mg/100g, chickpeas: ~105 mg/100g). Total calcium from 2 tablespoons of this powder is estimated at **60–90 mg** — only **6–9%** of the 1,000 mg daily dose shown to reduce dysmenorrhea in clinical trials. Additionally, calcium bioavailability from whole seeds is lower than from supplements due to phytates and oxalates.

**3. Risk of Bias**
- **No studies cited** — the reel presents the claim as self-evident.
- **Study design**: This is effectively an anecdotal/traditional remedy recommendation — the lowest tier of evidence (Level 7: expert opinion/anecdote).
- The creator identifies as a "Nutritionist" — in India (where this content appears to originate), the title "nutritionist" is not legally regulated the same way "Registered Dietitian" is. No verifiable credentials, degrees, or certifications could be found.
- **Engagement incentive**: As a social media content creator, there is an inherent incentive to produce shareable, aspirational health content. No conflict of interest disclosure.

**4. Total Evidence**
- **Calcium and dysmenorrhea**: There IS legitimate evidence that calcium supplementation can reduce menstrual pain. A 2016 RCT (Zarei et al.) found that 1,000 mg/day calcium significantly reduced pain scores. A 2017 RCT (Shobeiri et al.) found 500 mg/day calcium reduced PMS symptoms. A 2024 systematic review (Amini et al.) confirmed an inverse relationship between calcium levels and dysmenorrhea severity.
- **However**, these studies used standardized calcium supplements at precise therapeutic doses (500–1,200 mg/day), NOT food-based powders delivering <100 mg. The dose delivered by this recipe is sub-therapeutic by a factor of 10–15x.
- **"Better energy"**: No clinical evidence supports this specific combination of ingredients for energy improvement around menstruation. Iron deficiency (not calcium deficiency) is the primary nutritional cause of menstrual fatigue.
- **"Better flow"**: No evidence base exists for calcium or any of these ingredients altering menstrual flow characteristics.
- **The 7-day window**: No study has tested a 7-day pre-menstrual calcium loading protocol. Studies showing benefit used daily supplementation over multiple menstrual cycles.

---

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade

### **Grade C** — Consensus, disease-oriented evidence, expert opinion, or case series only

**Rationale**: While calcium supplementation at therapeutic doses (500–1,000 mg/day) has Grade B evidence for dysmenorrhea, this specific claim fails on multiple fronts:
- The homemade powder delivers a sub-therapeutic calcium dose (~60–90 mg vs. the studied 1,000 mg)
- No clinical trial has tested this recipe or this specific combination of ingredients
- The "better energy" and "better flow" claims have no evidence basis
- The 7-day protocol has no clinical precedent
- The recommendation is based on traditional/folk remedy reasoning, not clinical outcome data

The underlying kernel of truth (calcium may help period pain) is real but is extrapolated far beyond what the evidence supports.

## Verdict
This reel contains a genuine kernel of truth — calcium supplementation has been shown in RCTs to modestly reduce menstrual pain — but dramatically over-promises on a homemade preparation that cannot deliver a therapeutic dose. Two spoons of this mixed powder provide roughly 60–90 mg of calcium, while clinical studies showing benefit used 500–1,000 mg of standardized calcium daily over multiple cycles (not just 7 days). The claims about "better energy" and "better flow" are entirely unsupported. Calling this a "calcium powder" is misleading when makhana and chickpeas — not calcium-rich foods — form the bulk of the recipe. The content omits allergen warnings, medical disclaimers, and any acknowledgment that severe period pain warrants medical evaluation. It is well-intentioned folk nutrition advice dressed up as medical guidance.
