## Summary
This is a plant-based recipe showcase carousel from Danielle Brown (@healthygirlkitchen) featuring 8 dishes with wellness-oriented marketing language. The recipes themselves appear nutritious and plant-based, but several image titles make implicit health claims — "stress relief mocktail," "longevity tacos," "gut-healthy" — that range from well-supported general advice to unsubstantiated specific claims. The overall message to "eat more plants" is backed by strong evidence, but the specific functional labels on individual dishes are marketing, not science.

### SORT Analysis (EBM)

#### Claim 1: "Stress Relief Mocktail"
- **SORT Grade: C**
- **POEM vs DOE**: No patient-oriented evidence that this specific drink relieves stress. This is a DOE-at-best claim (some ingredients like cucumber contain magnesium, a surrogate marker).
- **Evidence**: One RCT (PMC12239509) found cucumber supplementation showed *trends* toward improved mood/anxiety/sleep, but the group-by-time interaction was not statistically significant. Healthline and BBC reporting confirm there is **no scientific evidence** that "cortisol mocktails" lower cortisol levels. The psychological ritual of making a calming drink may contribute more than the ingredients.
- **Bias lens**: The term "stress relief" is a wellness marketing label, not a clinical claim. No dosage, mechanism, or evidence is cited.
- **Safety lens**: Low harm — it's a fruit/vegetable drink. Risk is in overpromising mental health benefits from a beverage.

#### Claim 2: "Eat More Plants"
- **SORT Grade: A**
- **POEM vs DOE**: POEM — multiple meta-analyses show plant-based diets reduce all-cause mortality (HR = 0.84–0.85, 95% CI: 0.79–0.90) across 11–14 prospective cohort studies (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2024–2025).
- **Evidence**: Consistent, high-quality patient-oriented evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Healthy plant-based diet adherence reduced cardiovascular mortality by 15–19%.
- **Caveat**: The quality of plant-based food matters. Unhealthy plant-based patterns (refined grains, sugary drinks) *increased* mortality risk (RR = 1.20). The advice is valid only if "plants" means whole, minimally processed plant foods.

#### Claim 3: "Longevity Tacos"
- **SORT Grade: B (for the underlying concept) / C (for the specific dish)**
- **POEM vs DOE**: The general concept that plant-based eating supports longevity is backed by POEM-level evidence (see Claim 2). However, labeling a specific taco recipe as promoting "longevity" extrapolates population-level dietary pattern data to a single meal, which is unsupported.
- **Evidence**: No study has tested whether any specific taco recipe extends lifespan. The ingredients (sweet potato, black beans) are individually nutritious, but the "longevity" label is marketing, not evidence-based.

#### Claim 4: Recipes Are "Functional, Nutrient Dense, Gut-Healthy"
- **SORT Grade: B (gut health) / C (functional)**
- **POEM vs DOE**: Gut microbiome composition is a DOE (surrogate marker), not a POEM. A 2023 systematic review of 12 interventional studies (PMC10057430) found plant-based diets had beneficial short-term effects on gut microbiota, including increased microbial diversity and short-chain fatty acid production. However, long-term POEM outcomes from these gut changes are not established.
- **"Functional"** is a marketing term with no standardized definition. It implies the food does something beyond basic nutrition but provides no specifics.
- **"Nutrient dense"** is plausible for whole-food plant-based dishes with diverse vegetables, legumes, and grains, but is not verified for these specific recipes without nutritional analysis.

#### Claim 5: "Protein Stuffed Pitas"
- **SORT Grade: Not gradeable (nutritional composition claim, not a health intervention)**
- The pitas appear to contain a bean or lentil filling, which are plant-based protein sources. Legumes typically provide 7–9g protein per half cup. Whether the pitas qualify as "high protein" depends on total protein content, which is unstated. Plausible but unverified.

#### Claim 6: NYT Bestselling Cookbook
- **Verdict: TRUE**
- Verified via Penguin Random House, Goodreads, LinkedIn, and PR Newswire. "HealthyGirl Kitchen: 100+ Plant-Based Recipes to Live Your Healthiest Life" is confirmed as a New York Times Bestseller, published May 2023.

## Verdict

This carousel is primarily a recipe showcase with wellness-flavored marketing language rather than a source of medical misinformation. The core message — eat more whole-food, plant-based meals — is strongly supported by Grade A evidence from multiple meta-analyses showing reduced all-cause mortality. However, several individual dish labels ("stress relief," "longevity," "gut-healthy") borrow clinical language to market recipes without citing or providing evidence. The "stress relief mocktail" claim is the weakest (Grade C) — there is no evidence a single green drink relieves stress. The "longevity" label extrapolates valid population-level data to a single dish, which is misleading. The NYT Bestseller claim is verified true. Overall, the content is **low risk** but uses health-halo marketing that could give viewers an inflated sense of specific health benefits from individual recipes. Safe to share with the caveat that the wellness labels are branding, not clinical claims.
