## Analysis

### Claim Validation

1. **"Air fryer uses rapid air circulation to crisp food with a little bit of oil (or oil spray) instead of litres of oil."**
Verdict: **Supported.** This is the textbook mechanism of forced-convection countertop ovens marketed as air fryers. Lab measurements (e.g., Teruel et al., LWT 2015; Sansano et al., J. Food Science 2015) show 70–80% reduction in fat content vs. deep-frying for potato products, with comparable acceptance in sensory panels. Acrylamide (a byproduct of high-temp starch browning) is *lower* than deep-frying but not zero — Gadre doesn't mention this, but also doesn't claim air-frying is risk-free, so not misleading.

2. **"Hand blender for dressings, chutneys, smooth soups in a jiffy."**
Verdict: **Not a claim, just a use case.** Nothing to validate.

3. **"Multi-cook steamer lets you steam multiple items simultaneously, saving electricity and time."**
Verdict: **Supported (trivially).** Stacked steaming is a standard food-science principle; single heat source serving multiple compartments reduces per-item energy. No nutrition claim attached (she does not, for instance, claim steaming preserves more nutrients than pressure-cooking — which would have been contested).

4. **"Food processor helps knead dough, chop in bulk, make chutneys — reduces labour of healthy eating."**
Verdict: **Supported.** Behavioral-nutrition research (Wolfson & Bleich, Public Health Nutr. 2015; Mills et al., Public Health Nutr. 2017) consistently finds that perceived time/effort barriers predict lower home-cooking frequency and higher ultra-processed-food intake. Tools that cut prep friction plausibly raise home-cooking adherence. This is soft behavioral claim, appropriately framed.

5. **"Vegetable chopper — kids can use them; speeds chopping."**
Verdict: **Use case, no science claim.**
