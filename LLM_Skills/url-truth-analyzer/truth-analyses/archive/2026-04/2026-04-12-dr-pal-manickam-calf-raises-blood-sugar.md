# Truth Analysis: Calf Raises Reduce Blood Sugar Spikes by 52%
**Source URL**: https://www.instagram.com/reel/DT-VWqlEwvp/
**Analyzed**: 2026-04-12
**Content type**: Medical
**Format**: Video (Instagram Reel)

**Share?**: Yes, with caveats — the underlying Hamilton study is real and published, but the 52% figure comes from a single lab study with continuous multi-hour soleus activation, not brief calf raises after meals. The practical benefit will be smaller.

## Summary
Dr. Pal Manickam presents research on the soleus muscle (deep calf muscle) and its ability to reduce post-meal blood sugar spikes. He claims calf raises can reduce glucose spikes by 52% (vs 30% from walking), cites the soleus as metabolically special due to slow-twitch fibers, and provides instructions for proper soleus activation: slow, continuous, controlled seated heel lifts.

## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

#### Safety Lens
The intervention (seated calf raises) carries essentially zero risk. No drugs, supplements, or invasive procedures. The reel explicitly states this does not replace walking. No absolute risk numbers for diabetes outcomes are provided — only relative glucose reduction percentages. No contraindications mentioned, though people with calf or Achilles injuries should exercise caution.

#### Outcomes Lens
**DOEs only — no POEMs**:
- 52% reduction in postprandial glucose excursion (DOE — lab glucose measurement)
- 60% reduction in insulin requirement (DOE — from the same Hamilton study)
- 30% glucose reduction from walking (DOE)

No patient-oriented outcomes are cited: no data on diabetes incidence reduction, HbA1c improvement, cardiovascular events, or mortality. The Hamilton study measured acute metabolic responses over 3 hours, not long-term clinical outcomes. Maximum SORT grade is B when only DOEs are present.

#### Risk of Bias Lens
**The Hamilton 2022 study** (*iScience*, DOI: 10.1016/j.isci.2022.104869):
- Small sample size (25 participants in the main study)
- Lab-controlled conditions: participants performed continuous soleus pushups for **hours** (not brief post-meal sets)
- Single research group — the findings have not been independently replicated
- The 52% figure specifically refers to glucose area-under-curve reduction during 3 hours of continuous soleus activation after a glucose tolerance test — not after a normal mixed meal
- No industry funding disclosed; funded by NIH

**The 30% walking claim**: Broadly supported by multiple RCTs and a 2023 systematic review with meta-analysis (Buffey et al., *Sports Medicine*). However, the specific "30%" figure is a rough summary — actual reductions vary by meal composition, walk duration, and timing (10–40% range across studies).

**Key overstatement**: The reel implies that brief calf raises after meals produce a 52% reduction. The actual study protocol involved continuous, uninterrupted soleus activation for multiple hours in a lab setting. The translation to "do calf raises after a meal" significantly overstates what the evidence demonstrates in real-world conditions.

#### Total Evidence Lens
- The soleus pushup concept is genuinely novel and the Hamilton study is real, peer-reviewed science
- However, it remains a single study from a single lab — no independent replication exists
- The broader literature on post-meal exercise and glucose control (walking, resistance exercise) is well-established and supports the general principle
- The soleus-specific claim is an outlier in magnitude (52% vs typical 10–30% from walking/exercise) and has not been validated in free-living conditions

### SORT Grade: **B** — Limited patient-oriented evidence; good-quality DOE from a single study

Rationale: The Hamilton study is well-conducted but small, unreplicated, and measured only disease-oriented outcomes (acute glucose/insulin curves). The 52% figure comes from lab conditions with continuous multi-hour activation, which the reel oversimplifies into "calf raises after a meal." The walking comparison (30%) is better supported but also approximate. The advice itself (do calf raises) is harmless and directionally correct, but the specific magnitude of benefit is overstated for real-world application.

## Verdict
Dr. Pal Manickam presents a real study (Hamilton 2022) accurately in some respects — the soleus muscle is genuinely metabolically unique and the 52% figure is in the paper. However, the practical translation is significantly overstated: the study involved continuous soleus activation for hours in a lab, not brief calf raises after a meal. The advice to do slow, controlled calf raises while sitting is harmless and likely provides some metabolic benefit, but expecting a 52% glucose reduction from casual post-meal calf raises is unrealistic. The comparison to walking (30%) is better supported by multiple trials. SORT Grade B: real science, oversimplified application. Best shared with the caveat that the magnitude of benefit in real life will be less dramatic than the headline number suggests.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
It's complicated. There IS a real study showing your calf muscle is weirdly good at burning blood sugar — that part's true. But the "52% reduction" came from people doing calf raises nonstop for HOURS in a lab, not a quick set after dinner. Will calf raises after a meal help? Probably a little. Will they cut your sugar spike in half? Nah. Walking after meals is still the better bet and has way more evidence behind it.
