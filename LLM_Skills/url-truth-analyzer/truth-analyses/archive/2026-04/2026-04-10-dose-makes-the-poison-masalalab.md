# Truth Analysis: The Dose Makes the Poison — Pay Attention to Denominators

**Source URL**: https://www.instagram.com/p/DWrE0QTiYWl/
**Analyzed**: 2026-04-10
**Content type**: General Science (food science / toxicology)
**Format**: Carousel (12 images)

**Share?**: Yes — this is one of the best science communication carousels on food safety scaremongering. Nearly every factual claim checks out, the dose-context framing is scientifically sound, and the nuance acknowledgments (e.g., "a few kids could be particularly sensitive") are honest. Two minor quibbles exist on specific numbers, but the overall message is accurate.

## Summary

Krish Ashok (@_masalalab) applies the foundational toxicology principle "the dose makes the poison" (Paracelsus) to six common food safety scares: aspartame causing cancer, MSG causing brain damage, acrylamide in air-fried food, food dyes causing ADHD, parabens causing hormonal disruption, and benzene in soft drinks/roti. For each scare, the carousel provides dose context — showing how much you'd actually need to consume to reach concerning levels — and argues that at real-world exposure doses, these substances are not meaningfully dangerous.

## Analysis

### Claim Validation

**Claim 1: "The dose makes the poison" — everything is toxic, conditions apply.**
**Supported.** This is the foundational principle of toxicology, attributed to Paracelsus (1493–1541): "All things are poison, and nothing is without poison; the dosage alone makes it so a thing is not a poison." It remains the cornerstone of modern regulatory toxicology and risk assessment.

**Claim 2: You'd need 17 cans of Diet Coke per day, every day, for life to hit the FDA safe limit for aspartame.**
**Supported.** The FDA ADI for aspartame is 50 mg/kg/day. A 12-oz Diet Coke contains ~180-200mg aspartame. For a 68 kg (150 lb) person: 3,400 mg / 200 mg = 17 cans. This calculation is well-documented by the FDA and independently verified. Note: the EFSA ADI is lower at 40 mg/kg, which would give ~13-14 cans for the same person. The post uses the FDA figure, which is standard for a US/global audience.

**Claim 3: IARC rated aspartame Group 2B — the same group as aloe vera and pickled vegetables.**
**Supported.** IARC classified aspartame as Group 2B ("possibly carcinogenic") in July 2023. Aloe vera extract and traditional Asian pickled vegetables are indeed also Group 2B. The post correctly implies that Group 2B reflects hazard identification (can it cause cancer in any theoretical scenario?) rather than risk (will it cause cancer at doses humans actually consume?) — a distinction the IARC itself emphasizes.

**Claim 4: A glass of milk has 6x more phenylalanine than a can of Diet Coke. Your body makes aspartic acid every day.**
**Supported.** Aspartame breaks down into phenylalanine, aspartic acid, and methanol. A glass of milk (~250ml) contains roughly 750mg phenylalanine vs ~120mg from a Diet Coke. Aspartic acid is a non-essential amino acid the body synthesizes endogenously. Both points are biochemically correct. (The phenylalanine comparison is relevant for the general population; phenylketonuria patients genuinely must avoid aspartame, which the post doesn't mention — a minor omission.)

**Claim 5: MSG — 1,680mg free glutamate in 100g Parmesan, 250mg in a ripe tomato, 22mg per 100ml breast milk, 0 upper limit set by FDA/WHO/EFSA.**
**Supported.** Parmesan has among the highest free glutamate concentrations of any food (~1,200-1,680mg/100g depending on age). Ripe tomatoes contain ~140-250mg/100g. Breast milk contains ~22mg/100ml of free glutamate. The FDA classifies MSG as GRAS (generally recognized as safe), and neither WHO/JECFA nor EFSA have established an upper intake limit ("not specified" ADI, which means no safety concern at normal intake levels). All figures check out.

**Claim 6: "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome" myth started from a joke letter to a medical journal in 1968.**
**Supported with nuance.** The letter was published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1968 by Dr. Robert Ho Man Kwok. Whether it was literally a "joke" is debated — recent investigative journalism (Colman Andrews, This is the Person Who Invented 'Chinese Restaurant Syndrome') suggests it may have been a satirical letter by Dr. Howard Steel under a pseudonym. Regardless of intent, the letter spawned decades of anti-MSG research that has consistently failed to confirm the syndrome in blinded controlled trials. The FDA's own commissioned review (FASEB, 1995) found no evidence of harm at normal intake levels.

**Claim 7: You'd need 40 kg of fries per day to hit the benchmark dose for tumour risk in mice for acrylamide.**
**Supported with caveat.** This figure is commonly cited in food science communication and derives from extrapolating the animal study benchmark dose (BMDL10) back to human dietary exposure. EFSA's 2015 scientific opinion confirmed that acrylamide margins of exposure indicate a concern for public health, but the concern is about population-level chronic exposure — not individual servings. The 40kg figure correctly illustrates the absurdity of acute panic, though EFSA does recommend minimizing acrylamide exposure as a prudent precautionary measure (ALARA principle). The post's framing that "humans have eaten it since we discovered fire" is factually correct — the Maillard reaction (carbs + asparagine > 120°C) has been part of cooked food since the origin of cooking.

**Claim 8: Food dyes — 6 dyes mixed with sodium benzoate in the Southampton 2007 study. 50-100 candies/day to hit Red 40 safe limit.**
**Supported with nuance.** The Southampton study (McCann et al., 2007) indeed tested mixtures of 6 artificial colors with sodium benzoate, making it impossible to attribute effects to any single dye. The EU ADI for Red 40 (Allura Red) is 7 mg/kg/day. The 50-100 candies figure depends on the specific candy and child's weight — it's a reasonable order-of-magnitude estimate. The post correctly notes "a few kids could be particularly sensitive" — the EU now requires warning labels on foods with these dyes. Carmine (E120) is indeed made from cochineal insects.

**Claim 9: Parabens — 100,000x weaker estrogenic activity than the body's own estradiol. Darbre 2004 study didn't test non-cancerous tissue or establish causation. Para-hydroxybenzoic acid occurs in blueberries, carrots, cucumbers, barley.**
**Supported.** Published studies have measured paraben estrogenic activity at approximately 10,000x to 100,000x weaker than 17β-estradiol depending on the specific paraben (methylparaben is weakest, butylparaben strongest). The Darbre et al. (2004) study detected parabens in 18/20 breast cancer tissue samples but had well-documented limitations: no non-cancerous control tissue was tested, no healthy breast tissue comparison, and no causal link was established. The CIR (2010) and SCCS (EU) subsequently concluded parabens are safe at cosmetic-use concentrations. Para-hydroxybenzoic acid does occur naturally in the listed foods.

**Claim 10: Benzene — 1-5 ppb in most soft drinks tested. 10 ppb WHO safe limit. 10,000x more benzene from one cigarette than a can of soda.**
**Supported.** The FDA's 2005-2007 survey found most soft drinks contain <5 ppb benzene (many <1.5 ppb after reformulation). The WHO drinking water guideline is 10 ppb. A single cigarette yields ~6-73 µg benzene (mainstream smoke), while a soft drink with 5 ppb benzene in a 355ml can contains ~0.002 µg — making the cigarette exposure roughly 3,000-36,000x higher. The "10,000x" is a reasonable middle estimate. The claim about Indian traffic benzene exposure is directionally correct — urban traffic air in India contains high benzene levels (often 10-50 µg/m³) making commuter inhalation exposure vastly larger than dietary intake from beverages.

**Claim 11: Water kills at 6 litres. Oxygen is poisonous above 50%. Caffeine kills at ~10 grams.**
**Supported.** Water intoxication (hyponatremia) can be fatal at roughly 6 liters consumed in a short period (cases documented). Oxygen toxicity occurs at partial pressures above ~0.5 atm (roughly 50% at sea level) with prolonged exposure, causing pulmonary and CNS toxicity. Caffeine's LD50 in humans is estimated at ~150-200 mg/kg, which equals ~10-14g for a 70kg adult. All three figures are in the correct range.

## Verdict

This is excellent science communication. Krish Ashok applies the correct foundational toxicology principle — dose determines toxicity — and backs it with specific, verifiable numbers that are almost all accurate. The carousel covers six common food safety scares and in each case provides dose context that is consistent with positions held by the FDA, WHO, EFSA, and IARC. Minor points: the post doesn't mention that phenylketonuria patients should avoid aspartame, that EFSA does recommend minimizing acrylamide exposure under the precautionary ALARA principle, or that the Southampton food dyes study did find a statistically significant effect (even if the dose was far above normal). But these are nuance omissions in a format that necessarily simplifies — the core message ("pay attention to denominators before panicking") is scientifically sound and urgently needed in an era of food safety scaremongering.
