**Share?**: Yes. Two of the three quantitative claims are evidence-based; the third ("80-year-old lifter as strong as 30-year-old non-lifter") is a rhetorical flourish that's *sometimes* true for specific strength metrics in lifelong trained individuals, not a general rule. ## Summary
Dan Go (@CoachDanGo, fitness coach, ~1M+ followers) posts a single text card framing resistance training as a "retirement & insurance plan." Three claims:
1. **Muscle mass decreases ~3–8% per decade after age 30, faster after 50.**
2. **"Studies show that an 80-year-old who lifts weights regularly is as strong as a 30-year-old who doesn't."**
3. **"I'd rather be the oldest person in the gym than the youngest in the nursing home"** — motivational close, not a testable claim.

## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

**Claim 1 — "Muscle mass decreases ~3–8% per decade after 30, faster after 50."**
Evidence: Very well established.
- Volpi et al. Curr Opin Clin Nutr Metab Care 2004: cross-sectional data show ~3–8% per decade skeletal-muscle-mass loss starting ~age 30–40.
- Janssen et al. J Appl Physiol 2000 (NHANES III, n=18,000): skeletal muscle mass declines progressively after age 30, with sharper losses after 60.
- Mitchell et al. Front Physiol 2012: ~1% per year muscle-mass loss after 50, ~3% per year strength loss.
- AWGS 2019 & EWGSOP2 2019 sarcopenia criteria use essentially this trajectory as the clinical baseline.
**SORT: A** — consistent POEMs across large cross-sectional and longitudinal cohorts.

**Claim 2 — "An 80-year-old who lifts weights regularly is as strong as a 30-year-old who doesn't."**
Evidence: Partially supported, heavily simplified.
- Pollock et al. J Appl Physiol 1997 and Wroblewski et al. Phys Sportsmed 2011: masters athletes (40–81 yr) who continued resistance training maintained muscle mass and strength close to that of much younger sedentary adults; Wroblewski specifically showed preserved thigh muscle cross-sectional area in 40–81-yr-old athletes comparable to sedentary 20–30-yr-olds.
- Fiatarone et al. JAMA 1990: high-intensity strength training in 87–96-year-olds (!) roughly doubled leg strength in 8 weeks, demonstrating that trainability is preserved into very old age.
- BUT the specific claim "as strong as a 30-year-old who doesn't lift" depends heavily on which strength measure (1RM squat? grip? leg-press relative to body weight?) and which population (elite masters athletes vs. average gym-going seniors). General population 80-yr-old recreational lifter will typically *not* out-deadlift a sedentary 30-yr-old on absolute load, but may match or beat them on relative strength, functional tests (sit-to-stand, gait speed), and grip.
**SORT: B** — directionally correct and supported for some measures in trained older adults; overstated as an absolute.

**Claim 3 — "Start lifting weights so you can reap the rewards now and later on."**
Evidence: Strong consensus.
- Li et al. BJSM 2018 meta-analysis: muscle strength inversely associated with all-cause mortality.
- Saeidifard et al. Am J Prev Med 2019: resistance training ~10–17% lower all-cause mortality independent of aerobic activity, with ~30–60 min/week giving near-maximum benefit.
- WHO 2020 physical activity guidelines: adults should do muscle-strengthening ≥2 days/week; >65 adds balance/functional training.
- ACSM 2021 resistance training position stand.
**SORT: A** — consistent POEMs.

### Pass 2 — Overall Grade
**B+**. Claims 1 and 3 are Grade A; Claim 2 is Grade B (supported for trained elderly on relative/functional measures, overstated as a universal absolute-strength parity). No product sale, no supplement, no affiliate. The post's core practical takeaway — "sarcopenia is real, start resistance training" — is textbook-correct geriatric medicine.

### Risks of bias
Dan Go is a fitness coach whose business depends on clients valuing resistance training. That's aligned, not conflicted — he would be the same if the science disagreed, but it doesn't, so the alignment here is benign. No affiliate link or supplement upsell in this specific post.

## Verdict
A motivational single-slide that hits above its weight. The "3–8% per decade" figure and the "lift weights to live longer and stay independent" thesis are both thoroughly evidence-supported. The catchy "80-year-old lifter = 30-year-old non-lifter" line is closer to a masters-athlete *study* than a universal truth — but it's in the right zip code. Safe to share with anyone in their 30s/40s/50s who's been putting off starting to lift.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
Yes, take this one to heart. You genuinely do start losing muscle in your 30s, and it accelerates as you get older — that's why grandparents get frail, not because "old = frail" but because nobody's been lifting. People who keep resistance training into their 70s and 80s stay dramatically stronger and more independent than peers who don't. The "80-yr-old = 30-yr-old" line is a little hyped, but the bigger point is 100% real: lifting weights is the closest thing to a retirement plan for your body.
