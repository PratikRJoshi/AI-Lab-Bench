# Truth Analysis: Bioavailability — Why Spice Molecules Aren't Medicine
**Source URL**: https://www.instagram.com/reel/DW4Ez5OCcW0/
**Analyzed**: 2026-04-13
**Content type**: Medical
**Format**: Video (Instagram Reel)

**Share?**: Yes — this is an outstanding pharmacokinetics explainer that correctly dismantles supplement marketing by introducing the single most important concept most people have never heard of: bioavailability. Every dose figure and mechanism checks out.

## Summary

The reel introduces the concept of bioavailability — the percentage of an ingested compound that reaches the bloodstream in active form. It argues that curcumin (turmeric), resveratrol (red wine), EGCG (green tea), and thymoquinone (kalonji/nigella seeds) all show impressive effects in vitro but have extremely low oral bioavailability (<1% for most), meaning dietary doses from food/spices are orders of magnitude below what clinical trials use. Even the piperine/black pepper "hack" for curcumin (2000% boost) still leaves you far short of therapeutic doses. The thesis: spices are great in food, but they aren't medicine, and supplement companies exploit this knowledge gap.

## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Analysis Lenses

**Safety**
The reel explicitly warns against dangerous overconsumption: "please do not try" drinking 3 tablespoons of turmeric; notes that 100 glasses of wine would cause liver failure from alcohol before resveratrol helps; and flags that green tea supplements are "likely to cause liver damage." These safety warnings are well-calibrated — USP's comprehensive review documents EGCG hepatotoxicity at 140–1000 mg/day with a hepatocellular injury pattern. The reel correctly identifies risk without quantifying it in absolute numbers, which is appropriate for a short-form format.

**Outcomes**
The reel does not claim any patient-oriented outcomes (POEMs). Instead, it correctly identifies that the cited compound effects are disease-oriented evidence (DOEs) from in vitro studies — "spectacular things in a petri dish" — and argues they don't translate to clinical benefit at dietary doses. This is a meta-analysis of the evidence quality itself, not a treatment recommendation. The critical insight — that in vitro efficacy ≠ clinical efficacy due to bioavailability — is one of the most important concepts in translational pharmacology.

**Risks of Bias**
The reel correctly identifies the bias source: supplement companies and influencers who cite in vitro data without acknowledging the bioavailability gap. This is a well-documented issue in nutraceutical marketing. The reel does not cite specific studies by name but references "clinical trials" generically. The pharmacokinetic claims are verifiable against published literature (see below).

**Total Evidence**
The bioavailability claims are consistent with the pharmacokinetic literature:

- **Curcumin <1% bioavailability**: Confirmed. Anand et al. (2007) and multiple pharmacokinetic reviews document that oral curcumin undergoes rapid first-pass hepatic and intestinal metabolism via glucuronidation and sulfation. Plasma levels of unconjugated curcumin remain below 2 nM even at 2280 mg doses with enhanced formulations (PMC 2024 review).
- **Piperine 2000% boost**: Confirmed. Shoba et al. (1998) in *Planta Medica* found exactly this figure in human volunteers given 2g curcumin + 20mg piperine. The mechanism: piperine inhibits hepatic and intestinal glucuronidation.
- **Turmeric is 3% curcumin by weight**: Confirmed. Standard turmeric powder contains 2–5% curcuminoids by weight (Prasad et al., 2014).
- **17g turmeric for low-end clinical dose**: Mathematically correct. 500mg curcumin ÷ 3% = 16.7g turmeric powder. At <1% bioavailability, only ~5mg would reach the bloodstream.
- **Clinical trials use 500mg–2g curcumin**: Confirmed. RCTs for osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis use 180mg–1000mg of bioavailability-enhanced curcumin formulations (Theracurmin, Curcuwin Ultra+), with standard trials using 500mg–2g of curcuminoids.
- **Resveratrol <1% bioavailability**: Confirmed. Walle et al. (2004) in *Drug Metabolism and Disposition* showed that despite ~70% absorption, only trace amounts (<5 ng/ml) of unchanged resveratrol appear in plasma due to rapid sulfate and glucuronide conjugation.
- **Red wine has ~1.5mg resveratrol per glass**: Approximately correct. Studies show 0.03–1.57 mg per 150ml glass depending on variety, with Pinot Noir at the high end (~1.57mg). The 1.5mg figure is in the upper range but reasonable for a high-resveratrol wine.
- **Clinical trials use 150–1000mg resveratrol**: Confirmed. Human trials typically dose at 150–500mg, with some going up to 5g.
- **100 glasses to match trial dose**: Mathematically reasonable. At 1.5mg/glass, 100 glasses = 150mg (matching the low end of clinical trials). At the average of ~0.3mg/glass, you'd need 500+ glasses.
- **EGCG <1% bioavailability and liver damage risk**: Confirmed. EGCG undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism. USP's 2020 comprehensive review (PMC7044683) documents hepatotoxicity at 140–1000mg/day with dose-dependent hepatocellular injury.
- **Thymoquinone requires concentrated oil, not a pinch of seeds**: Supported. Oral bioavailability of thymoquinone is ~58% (better than the others), but the concentration in whole seeds is low. Nigella sativa oil contains 0.5–3.7% thymoquinone, meaning meaningful doses require concentrated oil or supplements, not culinary seed quantities.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade

**Grade A** — The pharmacokinetic claims about bioavailability, first-pass metabolism, and clinical trial dosing are consistent with good-quality evidence from well-conducted pharmacokinetic studies (Walle 2004, Shoba 1998), systematic reviews of curcumin formulation trials (PMC 2023), and the USP comprehensive hepatotoxicity review (2020). The core argument — that in vitro efficacy doesn't translate to clinical benefit without adequate bioavailability — is a foundational principle of pharmacology, not a contested claim. The specific numerical claims (doses, percentages, bioavailability figures) are all verifiable against published human pharmacokinetic data.

Note: Grade A is unusual for a social media reel. It applies here because the reel is not making a novel treatment claim — it's explaining established pharmacokinetic science that is well-supported by consistent, high-quality evidence. The "recommendation" is essentially: don't confuse food-dose exposure with therapeutic-dose exposure, which is consensus pharmacology.
