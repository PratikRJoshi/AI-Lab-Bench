## Analysis
### Medical: SORT Analysis

**Pass 1 — Four lenses**

1. **Safety lens** — The post shows pills in a palm and lists no dose, no brand, no contraindications, no interactions, and no warning about ashwagandha-associated liver injury. The NIH LiverTox database lists ashwagandha as a documented cause of idiosyncratic drug-induced liver injury, with multiple case series published 2020–2023 (Iceland, Italy, US). Magnesium is generally safe below the 350 mg/day supplemental upper limit but causes diarrhea at higher doses and accumulates dangerously in renal impairment. None of this is mentioned. Pure "benefits" framing with zero harm disclosure is a major safety-communication failure.

2. **Outcomes lens** — The overlay names no outcome at all ("benefits of…"). Common claims for this stack are (a) reduced stress/anxiety, (b) improved sleep, (c) better workout recovery, (d) testosterone/strength gains in men. These are a mix of POEMs (subjective stress, sleep quality) and DOEs (serum testosterone, cortisol). The reel itself does not commit to any specific outcome, which makes the claim non-falsifiable and unscorable on its merits.

3. **Risk of bias lens** — Existing ashwagandha RCTs are predominantly small (n=40–120), short (8–12 weeks), conducted in India, frequently funded by the supplement industry, and use proprietary extracts (KSM-66, Shoden). Heterogeneity in extract, dose (300–600 mg/day), and outcome scales (PSS, HAM-A, PSQI) is high. Combined magnesium + ashwagandha RCTs in adults are essentially absent from PubMed; no head-to-head or factorial trials exist to support a "stack" claim.

4. **Total evidence lens** — No major guideline body (USPSTF, NICE, AAFP, Endocrine Society) recommends ashwagandha for any indication. The American Academy of Neurology gives magnesium a Level B recommendation for migraine prevention; Cochrane's 2020 review (Garrison et al.) found magnesium does not meaningfully reduce skeletal muscle cramps. There is no consensus body that endorses the magnesium-plus-ashwagandha pairing as shown.

**Pass 2 — SORT grade: C**

Justification: The on-screen claim is a generic, outcome-free supplement promotion. Even when steel-manned to "ashwagandha and magnesium have benefits": (i) ashwagandha's anxiety/sleep evidence is Grade B at best (small short RCTs, mostly industry-funded, narrow extracts), (ii) magnesium's strongest indication (migraine prophylaxis) is Grade B and is not what's implied here, and (iii) there is no combined-stack evidence at all, which is the actual product being shown. Add the omitted hepatotoxicity warning and the absence of dose/population specifics, and the post functions as Grade C consumer marketing.
