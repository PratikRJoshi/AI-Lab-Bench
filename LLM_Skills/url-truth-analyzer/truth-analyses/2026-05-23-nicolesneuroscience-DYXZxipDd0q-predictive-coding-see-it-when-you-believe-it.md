A 9-slide carousel arguing that perception is constructive, not receptive: the brain runs predictions, prioritises evidence that confirms them (so expecting rejection makes you find rejection), and you can alter what you perceive by deliberately altering what you expect. ## Analysis

### Claim Validation

**Claim 1 (Slide 2): "Your brain sends more signals from your brain to your eyes than the other way around, which means your brain is constructing reality based on what it expects to see."**
- **Status: Contested as stated, supported in spirit.**
- The literal claim is misleading. The optic nerve carries ~1 million ganglion-cell axons feedforward from retina to brain; centrifugal projections *from* brain *to* retina exist in some species but are negligible in primates. The brain does not, in any meaningful sense, "send more signals to your eyes." What Vignola is gesturing at is the well-established finding that within the visual system (LGN ↔ V1, and feedback from higher cortex to V1), **feedback (top-down) connections substantially outnumber feedforward (bottom-up) ones** — Sherman & Guillery showed >90% of synapses onto LGN relay cells are modulatory feedback from cortex, not retinal drivers. So the underlying point — that perception is dominated by top-down prediction — is correct; the "brain → eyes" phrasing is a compression error that misleads on anatomy.
- Caveat: this slide is the single biggest factual stretch in the carousel.

**Claim 2 (Slides 1, 6, 8, 10): "The brain doesn't just passively receive reality, it actively constructs it" / "The brain updates reality based on what it predicts and integrates, not just what is objectively true" / "Change the prediction, change the reality your brain notices."**
- **Status: Supported.** This is a fair restatement of the **predictive coding / active inference** framework that is now the dominant theoretical lens in cognitive neuroscience (Rao & Ballard 1999; Friston 2005; Clark 2013). Perception is modelled as Bayesian inference: the cortex generates predictions, compares them against sensory input, and updates beliefs on the prediction error. Vignola is simplifying but not misrepresenting.
- Caveat: the slogan "change the prediction, change the reality" understates how effortful belief updating actually is. Priors are sticky precisely *because* high prior precision is what makes predictive systems work — that's also why CBT, exposure therapy, and behavioural change take weeks-to-months, not a single conscious decision.

**Claim 3 (Slide 7): "If you stroke a rubber hand and your real hand simultaneously, within minutes the rubber hand will begin to feel like yours."**
- **Status: Supported.** This is the classic **rubber-hand illusion (RHI)** from Botvinick & Cohen (Nature, 1998). The effect is real, robust, and replicated hundreds of times. Onset is typically within **10–60 seconds** of synchronous visuo-tactile stroking — "within minutes" is accurate, even slightly conservative. The image she uses is the standard McGill teaching diagram (setup + synchronous-stroking panels).
- Caveat: roughly ~25% of participants do not experience the illusion, and asynchronous stroking abolishes it — both omitted on the slide, but neither undermines the claim.

**Claim 4 (Slide 9): "If you expect rejection, danger or failure, your brain starts prioritising evidence that confirms it. It'll see it because it already believes it."**
- **Status: Supported in spirit, oversimplified.** Two well-established phenomena converge here: (a) **confirmation bias** in reasoning and memory (Wason 1960; Nickerson 1998), and (b) **expectation/attention bias** in perception, where pre-stimulus expectation modulates early visual cortex activity (Kok et al., Neuron 2012). Anxiety and depression are also well-documented to bias threat-related attention. So the direction of the claim is correct.
- Caveat: "It'll see it because it already believes it" overstates determinism. Expectation biases *what is attended to* and *how ambiguous input is interpreted*, but it does not literally fabricate stimuli. The slide rhetorically slides from "perception is biased" toward "perception is invented" — those are different claims.

**Claim 5 (Slides 11–12): Prescriptions — challenge old assumptions, expose yourself to new experiences/people/environments to update predictions.**
- **Status: Supported as general advice.** Deliberate exposure to disconfirming evidence is the mechanism behind cognitive reframing, exposure-based CBT, and broadly the way predictive systems update priors. There is no specific peer-reviewed citation needed because this is a domain-general consequence of the framework she has already laid out — it is internally consistent rather than an empirical claim.
- Caveat: framed as a self-help action item, the carousel skips that durable prediction-change typically requires *repeated, emotionally salient, prediction-error-generating* experiences — not just a single "try something new" decision.

## Verdict
Substantively trustworthy as a pop-neuroscience primer, with one notable phrasing problem. The carousel's spine — predictive processing, the rubber-hand illusion, expectation-biased perception, and exposure-based belief updating — maps cleanly onto mainstream cognitive neuroscience and is correctly cited (implicitly) to the right body of work. The single weak link is the Slide 2 "brain sends more signals to your eyes than the other way around" line, which mis-localises a real intracortical-feedback finding onto eye-brain anatomy where it doesn't apply. The "change the prediction, change reality" prescriptive slides are directionally right but understate how stubborn priors actually are. Net: a competent and largely accurate explainer that earns its credentials, with the usual Instagram-format trade-off of compressing probabilistic claims into absolute slogans.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
Mostly legit. Your brain really does *guess* what's out there and then check those guesses against your eyes — that's actual neuroscience, not vibes. The rubber-hand thing she shows is a famous real experiment. The "brain sends signals to your eyes" line is the one bit I'd ignore — what she means is "your brain talks to itself about what you're seeing way more than your eyes talk to your brain," which is true; the way she said it is a little off. And the "just change what you expect" advice is fine in spirit, but in real life it takes a lot more than one decision — that's why therapy and habit change are slow. Solid scroll, just don't quote slide 2 at a neuroscience grad student.
