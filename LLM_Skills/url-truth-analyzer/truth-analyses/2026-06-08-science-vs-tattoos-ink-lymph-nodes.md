# Truth Analysis: Science Vs — "You're tattooing your lymph nodes too"

**Bottom line**: Accurate — tattoo ink really does migrate to and accumulate in lymph nodes (shown experimentally), and there's preliminary evidence linking tattoos to some cancers, but whether the ink *causes* harm is genuinely unknown — which is exactly how the clip frames it.

## Summary
Quoting a researcher: "When you get a tattoo, you are also tattooing your lymph nodes." Studies (including in mice) show ink travels to lymph nodes and can color them. Whether that's harmful long-term is unknown — it may relate to tattoo allergic reactions, or be innocuous. There is some evidence that people with tattoos are more likely to get some cancers, though it's not known whether the ink is the cause; the takeaway is "you're inking your lymph nodes, not just your skin."

## Analysis

### Science / EBM
Both the mechanism and the careful hedging are correct. Tattoo-ink pigment migrating to and depositing in regional lymph nodes is a documented phenomenon (pigmented "tattooed" lymph nodes have been observed in humans and demonstrated experimentally), and the melanoma study's authors explicitly hypothesize an **immunotoxicity / ink-migration-to-lymph-nodes** mechanism to explain the epidemiological cancer associations — noting the association was stronger for immunogenic cancers (melanoma, lymphoma) than for squamous cell carcinoma, and stronger still when excluding people on immunosuppressants [1]. Crucially, the clip does **not** claim causation: it says we don't know whether the ink is harmful, possibly linked to allergic reactions, possibly innocuous, and that the cancer association is real but unexplained [1]. That matches the evidence, where authors stress causality cannot be inferred and replication is needed.

## Verdict
All three claims **supported**: ink genuinely reaches lymph nodes (C1), tattooed people show higher relative risk of certain immunogenic cancers (C2) [1][2], and — importantly — the clip correctly says causation is unproven (C3). Responsible, non-alarmist framing of an open question. Safe to share. (See companion analysis on relative-vs-absolute risk for the same episode.) Citations verified directly.
