## Analysis

### SORT Analysis — Medical content

**Claim extracted from the thumbnail text overlay**

1. April Rodriguez's Hashimoto's was caused by the *combination* of (a) frequent residential moves during childhood, (b) daily antibiotics for over a year for teenage acne, and (c) chronic stress as a young adult — "all compounded and resulted in Hashimoto's."

This is a single composite etiological claim presented as a personal cause story but framed implicitly as a general lesson for the audience (the typical functional-nutrition "root-cause" rhetorical move).

#### Pass 1 — Four lenses

- **Safety.** The reel itself is low direct-harm: there is no advice to stop levothyroxine, no extreme protocol, no supplement pitch in this 6-second clip. The softer harms are (a) fostering causal certainty in viewers about their own etiology — Hashimoto's is ~70–80% genetic with multifactorial environmental modulation, so a "I figured out my root cause" narrative pushes followers toward retrospective self-diagnosis of triggers that may have nothing to do with their disease — and (b) priming them for a paid 1:1 coaching offer where the same narrative will be applied to them.
- **Outcomes (POEM vs DOE).** The "outcome" here is a diagnosis attribution, not a treatment outcome. As an outcome statement it is patient-oriented in form (developed Hashimoto's) but the *evidence* offered is purely autobiographical recall. There is no comparator, no quantification, no follow-up cohort — none of the structure that would let any of the three named factors be evaluated as a cause vs a coincident exposure.
- **Risk of bias.** Maximum for this kind of claim. Single self-reported case, retrospective recall, no controls, sourced from someone with a direct financial interest in clients believing they too can identify discrete "root causes" amenable to nutrition coaching. Classic post-hoc reasoning: every adult woman with Hashimoto's can produce a list of childhood/teen/young-adult exposures, and most lists will read as plausible because Hashimoto's onset spans the same age window during which most of life happens.
- **Total evidence.** Mapped against the broader literature, factor by factor:
  - **Frequent childhood moves**: Not in any established list of AITD risk factors (smoking, iodine intake, viral/bacterial infection, drugs, vitamin D / selenium status, irradiation, microbiota, stress, genetic load). I found no AITD literature treating residential mobility as an exposure of interest. As a mechanism it would have to act through childhood psychosocial stress, which itself is only weakly linked to Hashimoto's specifically (see below).
  - **Daily antibiotics for over a year (teen acne)**: Long-course oral antibiotics genuinely do alter the gut microbiome — the 2024 *Nature Medicine* Swedish cohort showed 10–15% of microbial species remain altered 4–8 years after exposure. Multiple systematic reviews (Frontiers Endocrinology 2021; IJMS 2022; Springer 2023) show that *gut microbiota composition is altered in Hashimoto's patients vs healthy controls* — for HT specifically, alpha diversity is often *higher*, not lower, complicating the simple "antibiotics → dysbiosis → AITD" story. None of those reviews establishes antibiotics as a causal exposure for HT in humans; the linkage is mechanistic and association-level, not interventional. Mori 2012 (*Discovery Medicine*) explicitly notes "few studies have examined whether antibiotics can influence Hashimoto's thyroiditis development."
  - **Chronic young-adult stress**: This is the strongest of the three but still only weakly supported for HT. The stress–AITD link is well established for *Graves' disease*; for HT it is "controversial" / "inconclusive" across multiple reviews (PMC11351723, 2024 review; PMC9917185, 2023; Markomanolaki RCT 2019). Some case-control work supports an association with early-life emotional stress (Sci Reports 2023, s41598-023-49993-3). Stress more clearly modulates *exacerbation and TPO antibody levels* than it does *new-onset Hashimoto's*.
  - **The compound claim** ("all compounded and resulted in"): Cannot be evaluated. There is no published study examining additive or interactive effects of these three exposures on AITD incidence. The phrasing is rhetorical, not scientific.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade

**Grade C** for the compound personal-etiology claim as presented.

- The claim is, structurally, an n=1 retrospective autobiographical attribution — exactly the type of evidence SORT places at the bottom of the bias hierarchy ("case series / case report / expert opinion / anecdote").
- One sub-component (chronic stress ↔ HT) has Grade **B/C** observational support (mixed across reviews, more robust for Graves' than HT) — the reel's framing exceeds what the evidence supports.
- One sub-component (long-course antibiotics → microbiome shift → HT) has Grade **C** — established mechanism for the first arrow, association-only for the second, no human interventional data for the chain.
- One sub-component (frequent childhood moves → HT) has effectively no direct evidence base and is best characterized as not addressed in the AITD literature.

## Verdict
This is a personal origin-story reel framed as a discovered causal mechanism. The on-screen text reads as a confident etiology statement — "all compounded and resulted in Hashimoto's" — but the underlying evidence base is uneven across the three named factors and absent for the compound claim. The strongest leg (chronic stress) has mixed observational support that's stronger for Graves' than for Hashimoto's; the middle leg (long-course teen antibiotics → AITD via microbiome) is mechanistically plausible and microbiome-altering effects of antibiotics are robust, but no human study establishes antibiotics as a causal trigger for Hashimoto's; the weakest leg (frequent childhood moves) has no AITD-specific evidence at all and at best operates as a vague proxy for childhood psychosocial stress. Treat the reel as a relatable personal narrative and a marketing hook for a functional-nutrition coaching business — not as evidence about what causes Hashimoto's. Standard practice (TSH/free T4/TPO monitoring, levothyroxine where indicated, address modifiable lifestyle factors as adjuncts) remains the floor; nothing in this clip overrides that.
