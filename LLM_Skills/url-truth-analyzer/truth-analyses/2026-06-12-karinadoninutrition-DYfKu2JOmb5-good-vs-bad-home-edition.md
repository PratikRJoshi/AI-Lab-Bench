## EBM SORT analysis

### C1 — "240,000 microplastic particles per liter of bottled water"
- **Verdict:** Numerically correct as a citation, but oversold and currently contested. **SORT B** (single high-profile DOE study, peer-reviewed letter raising methodological concerns).
- The figure traces directly to **Qian, Lang, Deng, et al., PNAS 2024** ("Rapid single-particle chemical imaging of nanoplastics by SRS microscopy"), which used hyperspectral stimulated Raman scattering on three popular US bottled-water brands and reported "about 2.4 ± 1.3 × 10⁵ particles per liter, about 90% of which are nanoplastics" — orders of magnitude higher than prior microplastic counts.
- **Important caveats the reel ignores:**
  - The authors themselves stated their seven-polymer reference library "can only account for roughly about 10%" of the imaged particles — so the rest are *unidentified* particles labelled nanoplastic by inference.
  - A peer-reviewed Letter in PNAS (Materić, 2024) flagged that without proper plastic-free blanks, ambient lab contamination cannot be excluded.
  - Health effects of nanoplastic exposure at these levels in humans are **not established** — this is a disease-oriented evidence (DOE) finding, not patient-oriented evidence (POEM). Capping at SORT B per the DOE-only ceiling.
- The reel's "approved bottle has close to nothing" claim is **unsupported**: no peer-reviewed test has compared specific commercial plastic bottles head-to-head and shown one with materially fewer nanoplastics. This is product endorsement framing, not data.

### C2 — "NSF-certified filters remove the majority of chemicals and heavy metals"
- **Verdict:** Directionally correct but conflates standards. **SORT B** (consistent consumer-protection guidance).
- NSF/ANSI **53** is the relevant standard for *health effects* — independently tested reduction of lead, hexavalent chromium, PFOA/PFOS, trihalomethanes, VOCs, arsenic (V), and cysts. NSF/ANSI **42** covers *aesthetic* claims (chlorine, taste, odor). NSF/ANSI **58** covers reverse-osmosis systems; **401** covers emerging contaminants (pharmaceuticals).
- "NSF certified" alone is not enough — the consumer must check **which NSF standard and which contaminants** the specific filter is certified for. Many cheap "NSF" filters carry only 42 (aesthetic) certification and will not reduce lead or PFAS.
- The reel's broad statement is true in spirit but vague enough to mislead a buyer into thinking any "NSF" sticker covers heavy metals. Caveat-needed.

### C3 — "Shower filter removes chlorine, better for hair, skin, and nails"
- **Verdict:** Plausible mechanism, weak clinical evidence, oversold for nails. **SORT B** for skin, **SORT C** for hair/nails.
- The strongest infant-skin signal comes from the **Perkin et al., J Allergy Clin Immunol 2016** cross-sectional study (n=1,303 three-month-olds) reporting an association between domestic water hardness and chlorine and atopic dermatitis risk in early life, with a follow-up gene–environment analysis (Jabbar-Lopez et al., 2020) and the **SOFTER** pilot RCT (Jabbar-Lopez et al., 2022) testing whether installing softeners before birth reduces eczema in genetically susceptible infants.
- Engebretsen et al. (2017) reported AD is more common in hard-water regions but population-attributable risk was only ~2%.
- For *adults* with intact skin barriers, evidence that a shower filter improves skin/hair outcomes is largely anecdotal. There is no peer-reviewed RCT that I can locate showing residential shower-filter installation produces clinically meaningful improvements in adult skin barrier function, hair structure, or *nails* (the nails claim is essentially marketing — nail plate is keratinized and not significantly affected by a shower's brief chlorine exposure).
- Bottom line: people with eczema or sensitive scalps may genuinely benefit from filtered or softened water; healthy adults will not see a dramatic effect, and "nails" is an unsupported add-on.

### C4 — "Tap water in America are terrible for you"
- **Verdict:** Misleading overgeneralization. **SORT B against** the blanket framing.
- US public water systems are regulated under the **Safe Drinking Water Act** via the EPA's National Primary Drinking Water Regulations — enforceable MCLs for ~90 contaminants including lead (action level 0.010 mg/L), the 2024-finalized **PFAS rule** (PFOA/PFOS at 4 ppt; PFNA/HFPO-DA/PFHxS at 10 ppt), disinfection byproducts (TTHM, HAA5), microbial pathogens, and radionuclides.
- Compliance is high in most large utilities; documented failures (Flint MI, Jackson MS, Newark NJ lead-service-line crises; widespread PFAS contamination near manufacturing sites; rural well-water nitrate issues) are real but *localized*.
- For most Americans, tap water is safer than the bulk of bottled water on every regulated parameter, and the EPA publishes annual Consumer Confidence Reports for each utility. "Terrible for you" as a national statement is not supported.
- A reasonable, evidence-based message would have been: *"Check your local Consumer Confidence Report; if you're on a private well or in a known PFAS/lead-affected area, install an NSF/ANSI 53 + 401 filter."* The reel chose the scarier, less specific frame.

### C5 — "Conventional cleaners are toxic, especially for babies and little kids"
- **Verdict:** Partly true; depends on product, dose, and route. **SORT C** (expert opinion / regulatory).
- Some household cleaners contain ingredients with documented respiratory or dermal hazards: ammonia, bleach (sodium hypochlorite, especially when mixed with acids → chloramine; or bleach + ammonia → chloramines), quaternary ammonium compounds (potential asthma trigger per several occupational-exposure cohorts), and concentrated VOC-emitting solvents.
- Pediatric vulnerability is real: poison-control calls for cleaning products are concentrated in children under 5 (US PCC annual reports), driven mostly by accidental ingestion / eye exposure, not normal household use.
- That said, "non-toxic" is **not a regulated term** in the US. EPA's Safer Choice and EWG's ratings are useful starting points, but the binary framing in the reel ("good vs bad") collapses dose, ventilation, and the actual hazard chemistry. A diluted bleach disinfectant used correctly is the WHO/CDC recommendation for many infectious-disease scenarios.

## Bottom line
This is a five-claim "good vs bad" rapid-fire reel where the headline numbers (240,000 microplastic particles/L; NSF certification) are real research touchstones, but each claim is over-generalized, stripped of caveats, and used to set up a "comment for my recommendation" lead-magnet to the creator's affiliate or product list. The strongest claim (microplastics in bottled water — Qian 2024) is itself contested in the peer-reviewed literature, and the broadest claim ("US tap water is terrible") contradicts EPA Safe Drinking Water Act compliance data for most utilities. Evidence-based take-aways for a viewer:

1. Drink tap water filtered through an **NSF/ANSI 53-certified** point-of-use device if you want to be belt-and-suspenders about lead, PFAS, and DBPs — that is genuinely good practice and matches mainstream public-health advice.
2. Bottled water is **not** automatically safer; the microplastic data, if it holds up, slightly disfavor it.
3. Shower filters are reasonable for people with eczema/sensitive skin or in hard-water regions; for healthy adults the benefit is small and the "nails" claim is unsupported.
4. Some cleaners genuinely warrant ventilation and child-safety, but "toxic" used as a uniform label is marketing language, not a chemistry assessment.
5. Treat this creator's nutrition certification as silent on water chemistry, dermatology, and toxicology — the credentials do not extend to those domains.
