## Analysis

### Claim Validation

**Claim 1: "One study is not the truth" — a single study should not be treated as definitive evidence.**

Status: **Strongly supported** — this is a foundational principle of evidence-based medicine and the scientific method.

The hierarchy of evidence places systematic reviews and meta-analyses above individual studies for precisely this reason. Individual studies can produce false positives, have methodological flaws, use small samples, or reflect population-specific effects that don't generalize. The SORT framework (Strength of Recommendation Taxonomy) explicitly requires "consistent, good-quality patient-oriented evidence" across multiple studies for a Grade A recommendation. A single well-designed RCT with patient-oriented endpoints only earns Grade B until replicated. The COVID-19 tocilizumab case is illustrative: eight small trials showed conflicting results before the large RECOVERY trial resolved the question — demonstrating that bodies of evidence, not single studies, drive reliable conclusions.

**Claim 2: "Science is a body of evidence, not a headline."**

Status: **Supported** — accurately describes the distinction between scientific consensus and media reporting of individual findings.

Health journalism routinely amplifies single-study findings with sensational headlines ("X causes cancer," "Y cures Z"), creating a whiplash effect where advice seems to reverse constantly. The actual scientific consensus on most topics moves incrementally as evidence accumulates. A 2023 Nature Medicine article specifically addressed how to "modernize medical evidence for the misinformation era," noting that systematic reviews exist precisely to synthesize contradictory findings from individual studies into actionable conclusions.

**Claim 3: Selectively citing one study while ignoring contrary evidence is called "cherry picking."**

Status: **Supported** — cherry picking is a well-documented cognitive and methodological bias.

Cherry picking (also: confirmation bias in study selection) is recognized in both formal research methodology and science communication. A 2023 PMC paper demonstrated mathematically that with a sufficiently large pool of studies, meta-analysts can falsely obtain significant or non-significant results by selectively including/excluding studies. A separate Journal of Clinical Epidemiology review confirmed that "cherry-picking by trialists and meta-analysts can drive conclusions about intervention efficacy." The concept is widely taught in critical thinking curricula, media literacy programs, and evidence-based medicine training.
