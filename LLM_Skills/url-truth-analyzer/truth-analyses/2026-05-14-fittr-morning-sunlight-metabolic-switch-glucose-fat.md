## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Lenses

**Safety**: No safety concerns. The recommendation to get morning sunlight is low-risk, free, and aligns with general health guidelines for circadian rhythm support.

**Outcomes**: The cited study (PMID 41418772, likely the Cell Metabolism 2025 paper by Harmsen et al.) reports both POEMs and DOEs:
- **POEM**: Time-in-range for glucose improved (59% vs 51% under artificial light) — this is a patient-relevant metric for T2D management
- **DOEs**: Increased whole-body fat oxidation, advanced skeletal muscle clock phase, elevated evening melatonin, altered serum metabolomics

**Risk of Bias**: 
- Published in *Cell Metabolism* (Impact Factor ~30) — one of the highest-quality metabolic journals
- Randomized crossover design (each participant serves as their own control — strong internal validity)
- Small sample (n=13) and short duration (4.5 days) — limits generalizability
- Single study, not yet replicated
- Independent academic research (Maastricht University) — no apparent industry funding conflict

**Total Evidence**: The circadian-metabolism connection is supported by a growing body of evidence. Systematic reviews confirm that light exposure timing influences glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and substrate oxidation. This specific study is well-designed but awaits replication. The broader mechanism (daylight → circadian alignment → metabolic improvement) is consistent with established chronobiology.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade

**Grade B** — Single good-quality RCT with patient-oriented outcomes (glucose time-in-range), published in a top journal. Not yet replicated across larger samples or longer durations. The claim is directionally correct and well-supported for a social media post.

## Verdict
This is a well-sourced health post that accurately represents a recent, high-quality RCT. The claim that morning sunlight improves glucose control and fat oxidation in T2D patients is supported by the cited evidence. The main caveat: the study is small (n=13) and short (4.5 days), so calling sunlight a "metabolic switch" oversells the current evidence slightly. But the direction is correct, the advice is free and low-risk, and citing the actual PMID is commendable transparency. This is what evidence-based social media health communication looks like when done reasonably well.
