## Summary
Aggie McCabe (@the.thyroid.rebel) lists five iron-and-ferritin tips for thyroid patients: (1) take iron on an empty stomach, away from coffee/tea and 4 h from levothyroxine; (2) prefer iron bisglycinate over GP-prescribed ferrous sulfate, with vitamin C; (3) dose every other day rather than daily; (4) eat steak/liver around menses; (5) avoid long-term unbalanced zinc supplementation because of iron-blocking and copper-depleting effects. Tips 1, 3, and 5 are well-supported by RCT and mechanistic evidence; tip 4 is reasonable food guidance; tip 2 oversells bisglycinate and undersells ferrous sulfate, and the "add colostrum" tag has only thin mechanistic backing.

## Analysis

### Medical: SORT Analysis

**Extracted claims (the 7-slide narrative, with files 1, 2, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 being the unique slides; files 3–7 are cross-promo cards for unrelated posts)**
1. Take iron on an empty stomach, ≥1 h away from coffee/tea, ≥4 h away from levothyroxine; pro tip — try taking iron before bed.
2. Don't take "GP-prescribed iron"; iron bisglycinate is gentler and "beats other forms"; take with vitamin C; "supercharge" with colostrum.
3. More is not better — alternate-day iron dosing is more effective than daily; she takes 2 × 20 mg iron bisglycinate every other day.
4. For menstruating thyroid patients: eat a big steak on day 1, liver on day 2 (or use liver-extract capsules).
5. Watch out for long-term zinc supplementation — it can block iron absorption and deplete copper, which is required for iron transport.

**Pass 1 — Four lenses**

- **Safety**: Mostly low-risk. Empty-stomach iron, vitamin C co-administration, and 4-hour spacing from levothyroxine match mainstream guidance. The "don't take the GP-prescribed iron" framing is the safety concern: ferrous sulfate / fumarate / gluconate are NICE/BNF first-line for iron-deficiency anaemia, are cheap, and work; nudging followers off the prescribed product without GP discussion can delay anaemia correction. Liver intake is iron-dense but also vitamin-A-dense — chronic high intake (especially in pregnancy or with retinol-containing supplements) carries a hypervitaminosis-A risk that the post does not flag. No NNT/NNH given anywhere.
- **Outcomes (POEM vs DOE)**: Endpoints in the underlying literature are mostly DOE-leaning (serum iron, ferritin, transferrin saturation, hepcidin AUC, fractional absorption) with some POEMs (fatigue scores, GI tolerability, time-to-normalised haemoglobin). Alternate-day dosing has both DOE (cumulative absorption) and POEM (fewer GI side-effects, comparable haemoglobin response) data.
- **Risk of bias**: The biggest claim — alternate-day > daily — is supported by independent academic groups (ETH Zürich, Stoffel/Zimmermann lab) using stable-isotope absorption studies and at least one randomised crossover in iron-depleted women, plus a real-world RCT in IDA. The bisglycinate-vs-sulfate literature is smaller and partly industry-funded; the colostrum claim is supported only by small mechanistic/lactoferrin studies.
- **Total evidence**: Tips 1, 3, 5 align with WHO, NIH ODS, and recent BSH/British Society for Haematology guidance. Tip 2's "bisglycinate beats other forms" is stronger than the systematic-review consensus (which is "comparable efficacy, possibly fewer GI side-effects, more expensive"). Tip 4 is consistent with dietary heme-iron biology but not a trialled protocol. Tip 1's pro-tip "take iron before bed" goes against typical guidance to take with vitamin C in the morning, but is reasonable for tolerability if no co-administered blockers.

**Pass 2 — Grade**

- Tip 1 (timing/blockers/levothyroxine spacing): **Grade A** — multiple RCTs and pharmacokinetic studies, plus guideline consensus.
- Tip 2 (bisglycinate "beats" sulfate; colostrum boost): **Grade B** for tolerability advantage of bisglycinate; **Grade C** for the "supercharge with colostrum" claim.
- Tip 3 (alternate-day dosing): **Grade B** (verging on A) — replicated RCT and stable-isotope evidence on absorption and haemoglobin recovery; some inconsistency on whether time-to-correction is faster, slower, or equal vs daily.
- Tip 4 (steak day 1, liver day 2): **Grade C** — sensible food framing, no trial of this exact cyclical protocol; vitamin-A safety caveat unaddressed.
- Tip 5 (zinc → iron block / copper depletion long-term): **Grade B** — well-documented zinc-iron and zinc-copper interactions; the magnitude depends on dose and timing.

Overall post grade: **B** — most factual content is consistent with current evidence; two specific framings (anti-prescription-iron, colostrum) are weaker than presented.

## Verdict
This is one of the more evidence-aligned posts in this niche. The top-line iron-handling tips — empty stomach, polyphenol blockers, levothyroxine separation, alternate-day dosing, awareness of zinc-iron-copper interactions — are reproducible takeaways from published RCTs and mainstream guidelines, and would not be out-of-place in a haematology clinic handout. The two slips are: framing GP-prescribed ferrous sulfate as something to actively avoid (it remains effective first-line therapy and is what the alternate-day RCT evidence is built on), and the "supercharge with colostrum" add-on, which sits on weak data and pulls the post toward supplement-stacking. Use the timing, dosing, and zinc-warning tips; check ferritin and treatment plan with your GP/endocrinologist before swapping prescribed iron for an OTC bisglycinate stack, and ignore the colostrum upsell.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
Most of what she says about iron pills is actually the same advice your GP or a haematologist would give: take it on an empty stomach, keep it away from your coffee, tea, and thyroid pill, and pair it with some vitamin C or lemon water. The "every other day, not daily" tip is a real research finding — your body absorbs more iron per pill when you space them out — and the warning about long-term zinc messing with iron and copper is also legit. Two things to skip: don't ditch the boring iron tablet your doctor prescribed just because Instagram says so (it works and it's cheap), and don't bother adding colostrum on top — that part's wishful thinking, not science.
