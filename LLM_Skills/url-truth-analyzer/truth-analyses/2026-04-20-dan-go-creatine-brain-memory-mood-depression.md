## Summary
Dan Go makes the case for creatine monohydrate beyond muscle-building: for brain energy, memory, focus, mood, and depression — especially under stress or sleep deprivation. Caption is appropriately hedged; the carousel slides themselves range from reasonable (Slide 3 anecdote about a friend doing dart-score math faster after upping creatine) to absolutist (Slide 4: "If you want to become a better human, take creatine").

## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

**Claim 1 — "Creatine may boost memory, focus, and mood, especially when stressed or sleep-deprived."**
Evidence:
- **Sleep deprivation**: Gordji-Nejad et al. Sci Rep 2024 — single high-dose (0.35 g/kg, ~25g for 70kg adult) creatine improved cognitive performance and brain energy markers after 24h sleep deprivation. Small n (15), acute paradigm, single dose — promising but preliminary.
- **Cognition in general**: Prokopidis et al. Nutr Rev 2023 meta-analysis (16 RCTs): creatine supplementation improved short-term memory and reasoning in healthy adults, most pronounced in older adults (65+) and vegetarians (lower baseline creatine stores). Effect sizes modest.
- **Under stress/sleep-deprivation**: McMorris et al. Psychopharmacology 2006 — creatine supplementation reduced cognitive and psychomotor performance decline after sleep deprivation.
**SORT: B** — inconsistent effect sizes across populations, POEMs available for older adults and sleep-deprived subgroups, less convincing in young well-rested adults. Evidence is *trending positive* but not yet definitive.

**Claim 2 — "Some studies link creatine to reduced depression and fatigue."**
Evidence:
- Kious BM et al. Nutrients 2019 review: biological plausibility (creatine kinase activity in brain, mitochondrial bioenergetics).
- Lyoo IK et al. Am J Psychiatry 2012: 5g/day creatine + SSRI (escitalopram) in women with MDD → faster and greater response than SSRI alone (small RCT, n=52).
- Roitman S et al. Bipolar Disord 2007: creatine augmentation in bipolar depression — small open-label, mixed results; one case of mania switch.
- Meta-analysis by Bakian AV et al. Transl Psychiatry 2020 (NHANES data, n=22,692): higher dietary creatine intake associated with lower prevalence of depression.
**SORT: B/C** — small RCTs + observational suggestive but not conclusive. Most psychiatric guidelines (APA, NICE) do not currently recommend creatine as a first-line or adjunctive treatment for depression. It's "hypothesis-generating with modest supporting data."

**Claim 3 — "Fuels brain cells, helping with energy and mental clarity."**
Evidence: Mechanistically supported. Brain creatine ↔ ATP buffering via creatine kinase; brain accounts for ~20% of BMR despite being 2% of body mass. Creatine supplementation raises brain creatine by ~5–10% measurable via MR spectroscopy (Dechent PLoS ONE 2006; Turner CE Ann Neurosci 2015).
**SORT: B** — mechanistic claim supported; clinical translation to "mental clarity" is shakier (DOE → POEM gap).

**Claim 4 (Slide 4) — "If you're depressed, take creatine. If you're sleep-deprived, take creatine. If you want to become a better human, take creatine."**
This is influencer-absolutist framing, not a clinical recommendation. Substituting creatine for evidence-based depression care (therapy, SSRIs, ketamine for TRD) would be genuinely harmful. "Become a better human" is marketing fluff.
**SORT: C / rhetorical** — not a defensible medical claim.

**Claim 5 — "Generally safe, but if you've got kidney issues or take related meds, check with your doctor."**
Evidence: Correctly hedged. Creatine in healthy adults at 3–5 g/day maintenance (or 0.1 g/kg/day) has an excellent long-term safety profile (Kreider RB ISSN 2017 position stand; 20+ years of trial data). Creatinine rises modestly (lab artifact, not kidney injury). Contraindicated/caution: pre-existing renal disease, concurrent nephrotoxins.
**SORT: A** — safety well-characterized.

### Pass 2 — Overall Grade
**B** for the caption-level framing. **C** for Slide 4's absolutism. The underlying pharmacology and emerging cognitive literature support creatine as a potentially useful supplement with low downside risk, but:
- Don't stop your antidepressants.
- Don't expect it to make you a genius.
- Effect sizes are modest and most pronounced in deficient populations (vegetarians, elderly, sleep-deprived).

### Risks of bias
Lead-gen funnel ("comment CREATINE for my free guide") is the standard engagement-farming pattern. Dan Go is a fitness coach, not a clinician or RD. No supplement brand is directly promoted in the post, but the "guide" likely funnels to affiliate links downstream.

## Verdict
Creatine-for-the-brain is one of those rare supplement stories where the biological plausibility is strong, the safety record is excellent, and the emerging clinical data are *genuinely promising* — but it's being oversold by the influencer ecosystem as a miracle cognitive enhancer and depression cure, which the evidence does not support. Caption is reasonably hedged; Slide 4's "if you're depressed, take creatine" is irresponsible if taken as "use this instead of treatment." For a healthy adult under 40 taking 3–5g/day creatine monohydrate, the downside risk is essentially zero and the upside is a modest cognitive edge especially if you're sleep-deprived, vegetarian, or older. For anyone clinically depressed, see a doctor.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
Partly right, partly hyped. Creatine really does get into your brain, and there's real (if modest) research showing it helps memory and cognition especially if you're sleep-deprived, vegetarian, or older. There are even small studies suggesting it *adds* to SSRI response in depression. Safety at 3–5g/day is rock solid after 20+ years of use. But Slide 4 — "if you're depressed, just take creatine" — is dangerous oversimplification; it's an *adjunct* candidate at best, not a replacement for actual mental-health care. If you're a generally healthy adult who wants to try it, it's one of the safer and better-supported supplements out there. If you're clinically depressed, still see your doctor first.
