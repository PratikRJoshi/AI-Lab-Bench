# Truth Analysis: People Are Disappearing in National Parks: Are Aliens Abducting Them?
**Source URL**: https://youtu.be/CdHyIbwCYpg
**Analyzed**: 2026-05-24
**Content type**: General Science (paranormal / cryptid genre)
**Format**: Video (~15 min, YouTube captions)

**Share?**: No — the rhetorical framing ("aliens abducting") is built on selection bias, base-rate neglect, and uncorroborated single-witness narratives. A handful of factual scaffolding claims (NPS has no central public missing-persons database; a real 1981 Yosemite case named Stacey Arras exists; FOIA exemption 7A can be invoked on cold cases) are accurate, but they are used as launchpads for paranormal inferences that the underlying data does not support.

## Summary
A ~15-minute Joe Rogan Experience clip with David Paulides, a retired police officer and self-publishing author of the "Missing 411" book/film series. Paulides recounts the 1981 Stacey Arras Yosemite disappearance, alleges obstruction by the National Park Service when he FOIAs old missing-persons files, and pivots into two paranormal/UFO anecdotes (a Washington State "elk abduction" witnessed by tree planters, and a Wyoming hunter named "Carl" who was allegedly taken aboard a craft, had his bullet deformed in mid-air, and had childhood tuberculosis scars erased). The clip mixes a real, well-documented record-keeping problem (no centralized NPS missing-persons database) with anecdotal abduction tales presented as evidence of a non-mundane pattern. The conventional explanations for "Missing 411" cases (hypothermia, falls, drownings, mental-health crises, animal predation, hiking solo without communications) are not engaged.

## Channel Reputation
The transcript opens with the audio bumper **"The Joe Rogan Experience"**, so this is a JRE excerpt — most likely the JRE Clips channel or a third-party reupload, not the main PowerfulJRE channel given the URL slug. The on-camera guest, **David Paulides**, is a former San Jose police officer turned paranormal author who runs CanAm Missing Project and the "Missing 411" book series (Missing 411: The Hunted, Missing 411: The Devil's in the Details, etc.). Paulides previously worked on Bigfoot research with the North American Bigfoot Search (NABS) before pivoting to missing-persons cases. His work is **self-published**, has **never undergone peer review**, and has been repeatedly criticized by SAR (Search and Rescue) professionals, skeptics, and the National Park Service for cherry-picking, omitting documented conventional outcomes, and framing statistically expected disappearances in vast wilderness as anomalous. JRE itself is the largest podcast in the world by reach, with a documented history of platforming paranormal, UFO, and fringe-medical guests without on-air pushback. **Treat factual claims in this clip as needing independent verification; treat the abduction anecdotes as uncorroborated single-witness narratives.**

## Analysis — Claim Validation

### Claim 1: "Stacey [Arras] disappeared at Yosemite at age 14, with her dad, on a horseback trip to a High Sierra camp ~46 years ago; lens cap found in treeline; never found."
**SUPPORTED (as basic facts).** Stacey Arras, 14, disappeared on **July 17, 1981** at Sunrise High Sierra Camp in Yosemite. She walked off to take photos with a 77-year-old fellow guest (Gerry Stuart), did not return, and a large multi-day search recovered only an item described in NPS records as a film-cartridge-style camera artifact near the trail. The case remains officially open and unsolved. Paulides recounts the basic outline correctly. The case is real; what it *means* is the contested part.

### Claim 2: "I FOIAed the Stacey Arras case file and was denied under FOIA exemption 7A — they said the criminal investigation is still ongoing 40+ years later, which is weird."
**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED, framing CONTESTED.** FOIA exemption 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A) does allow withholding of law-enforcement records when disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Federal agencies, including NPS, routinely keep cold homicide and unresolved-disappearance files coded as open indefinitely; this is **standard federal practice**, not unique to NPS, and is the same reason FBI cold-case files stay sealed for decades. It is consistent with both (a) genuine obstruction *and* (b) routine bureaucratic inertia + standard cold-case policy. Paulides presents only interpretation (a).

### Claim 3: "The National Park Service does not have a centralized public list of missing persons across its parks."
**SUPPORTED.** NPS has no public, centralized, queryable missing-persons database covering the entire park system. Each park maintains its own incident records; there is no unified equivalent of the FBI's NCIC public-facing search. This is a real and frequently criticized record-keeping limitation. **However, this is a record-keeping/IT problem affecting many decentralized federal land-management agencies (USFS, BLM, NPS), not active evidence of suppression or cover-up.** NamUs (the National Missing and Unidentified Persons System, run by DOJ/NIJ) does aggregate missing-persons data nationally and includes many park-related cases.

### Claim 4: "NPS quoted me $1.4 million for a full-system missing-persons list and $34,000 for Yosemite alone."
**UNVERIFIED but PLAUSIBLE.** FOIA agencies can charge requesters for search and review time when the request is large or commercial-use. Quoted estimates in the hundreds-of-thousands range have occurred for sprawling, multi-park requests requiring manual file pulls across decades. Without the actual FOIA correspondence, the specific dollar figures can't be checked, but the mechanism is real and not by itself evidence of bad faith.

### Claim 5: "Yosemite alone has over 50 missing people right now."
**CONTESTED / DEFINITIONAL.** Yosemite's own published cold-case list and aggregated reporting (Outside, SFGate, AP) typically cite figures in the **low-to-mid double digits** for cases that remain open across the park's ~135-year history, depending on how "missing" is defined (presumed-dead-but-no-body-recovered vs. actively unaccounted-for vs. those later determined to have left voluntarily). Yosemite receives **~3.5–4 million visitors/year**, so even ~50 unresolved cases over a century is a **vanishingly small rate** (~1 in millions of visitor-days). Paulides cites the absolute number without the base rate, which is textbook **base-rate neglect**.

### Claim 6: "I have 1,200 to 2,500 cases where canines couldn't track the missing person, which is outside normal dog behavior."
**REFUTED as framed.** SAR (Search and Rescue) literature is clear that **scent-tracking dogs frequently fail** to locate missing persons, especially after rain, after 24–48 hours, in high winds, on rocky/hard terrain, in cold conditions, or when the subject crossed water or roads. Documented K-9 success rates in wilderness SAR are commonly cited at **30–50%** under ideal conditions and lower in real-world deployments. Dogs failing to track is the *modal* outcome in many wilderness SAR scenarios, not an anomaly. Paulides presents the failures without any denominator (how many of the **total** Missing-411-style cases did dogs succeed on?), which is **selection bias**.

### Claim 7: "Every time on a missing-hunter case it's at the 'point of separation' that something happens — never when people are together."
**REFUTED (tautological / base-rate artifact).** Hunters and hikers who disappear are by definition people for whom no companion witnessed the disappearing event. If the companion *had* seen what happened, the person would not be classified as "mysteriously missing" — they would be classified as "fell off cliff," "drowned," "attacked by bear," etc. **The pattern is a definitional artifact of how 'missing' is operationalized, not a paranormal signature.** This is one of the most-cited criticisms of the Missing 411 methodology in the skeptical literature (e.g., Skeptoid, John Billingsley's reviews).

### Claim 8: "12 tree planters in Washington State witnessed a UFO levitate a single elk out of a herd; MUFON investigated and confirmed."
**UNCORROBORATED / REFUTED standard of evidence.** This anecdote appears in Paulides' film *Missing 411: The Hunted*. **MUFON (Mutual UFO Network) is a hobbyist UFO-enthusiast group, not a scientific or law-enforcement investigative body.** Mass-witness UFO claims of this type have well-documented psychological mechanisms (memory conformity, leading interviews, group reinforcement). No independent contemporaneous documentation (news report, USFS incident report, video) of the elk incident has surfaced. By scientific standards this is single-source anecdote, not evidence.

### Claim 9: "Carl, a hunter in Wyoming's Medicine Bow National Forest, saw his bullet drop in mid-air, was abducted by two alien entities via 'mind speak,' was returned, and his childhood tuberculosis scars were medically gone afterward."
**REFUTED on biology; UNCORROBORATED on narrative.** Tuberculosis lung scarring consists of **calcified granulomas** (Ghon complexes / fibrocalcific lesions) that are physically deposited calcium and fibrous tissue. These do **not spontaneously disappear** in adult lungs; they are visible on chest X-ray for life. A claim that they vanished is not biologically plausible without independent before/after radiology reviewed by a pulmonologist. The "bullet stopped in mid-air and deformed strangely" claim has no independently published forensic report. The full narrative matches the textbook structure of **abduction confabulation** (missing time, telepathic communication, medical examination, returned with anomalies), a pattern documented since the Hill case (1961) and analyzed extensively in sleep-paralysis and false-memory literature (Susan Clancy's *Abducted*, 2005).

### Claim 10: "Missing-person cases should not be classified as criminal investigations, so invoking FOIA exemption 7A is improper."
**MISLEADING.** Missing-persons cases can absolutely have a criminal component (suspected foul play, kidnapping, suspicious circumstances) and routinely do get worked by detectives/special agents. NPS classification practices may be over-inclusive or bureaucratically conservative, but the categorical claim that no missing-persons case is criminal is wrong on its face.

## Verdict
**Mixed-but-skewed-toward-misleading.** The clip contains a small core of accurate, mundane scaffolding — Stacey Arras is a real unsolved 1981 Yosemite case, NPS does lack a centralized public missing-persons database, FOIA cost estimates can be high, and exemption 7A is a real statute. On top of that accurate substrate, Paulides layers (a) **selection bias** (only cases that fit his criteria are counted), (b) **base-rate neglect** (no denominator of park visitors or successful SAR outcomes), (c) **definitional artifacts** sold as patterns ("point of separation"), and (d) **single-witness paranormal anecdotes** (the elk, Carl's TB scars) presented with the same epistemic weight as the FOIA documents. The "aliens abducting" framing is rhetorical clickbait that Paulides himself softens in-clip ("In my books, I've laid out facts and I let the viewer decide"). Conventional explanations for Missing 411 cases — hypothermia confusion / paradoxical undressing, falls into water or crevices in heavily wooded terrain that confound dogs, mental-health crises, undiagnosed medical events, predation — are well-documented in SAR literature (Kenneth Hill, Robert Koester's *Lost Person Behavior*) and would account for the great majority of these disappearances without invoking any non-mundane mechanism.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
There's a guy named David Paulides on Joe Rogan saying people vanish in national parks in ways that *seem* unexplainable — dogs can't track them, the disappearances cluster at weird times, the Park Service won't release the files, and oh by the way one of them woke up with his old TB scars healed after an alien encounter. The pieces that sound official are *kind of* real — Stacey Arras really did disappear at Yosemite in 1981, NPS really doesn't have one big public missing-persons list, FOIA fights over cold cases really do happen. But the spooky pattern he builds on top of that is mostly an artifact of how he counts: he only looks at cases that already seem weird, ignores the millions of people who hike parks every year without incident, and ignores that scent dogs failing in wilderness 24+ hours later is the *normal* outcome, not a paranormal one. The "alien took my bullet and healed my lungs" stuff is one person's story with zero independent records. If you came in skeptical, leave skeptical — and if you came in spooked, go read a real Search-and-Rescue post-mortem (Robert Koester's *Lost Person Behavior* is the textbook) and watch how mundane the explanations almost always are.
