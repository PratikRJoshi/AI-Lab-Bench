## Analysis

### EBM SORT-flavored claim review

**Claim 1 — "Creatine is the most studied supplement in the history of sports science" — Grade A (uncontroversial)**
True and frequently cited. There are 1,000+ peer-reviewed clinical studies and dozens of meta-analyses on creatine supplementation. The [International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) 2017 Position Stand on Creatine Safety and Efficacy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/) (Kreider et al., *J Int Soc Sports Nutr*) explicitly states this is the most-researched ergogenic aid.

**Claim 2 — "Creatine is not a drug, not a steroid; body makes ~1g/day in liver and kidneys; you get ~1g/day from meat or fish" — Grade A**
Mainstream biochemistry. Endogenous creatine synthesis from arginine, glycine, and methionine occurs primarily in the liver (and to a lesser extent kidneys/pancreas). Dietary intake is ~1–2 g/day for omnivores eating meat/fish. Anabolic steroids work via androgen receptor signaling — completely different mechanism class.

**Claim 3 — "Mechanism: phosphocreatine donates phosphate to ADP→ATP for ~10 seconds of all-out effort" — Grade A**
Textbook bioenergetics. The phosphocreatine system extends the immediate ATP availability beyond the ~2–3 seconds of stored ATP alone. The "10 seconds" framing is a reasonable simplification — in practice it's 8–12 seconds for the combined ATP-PCr system before glycolysis takes over.

**Claim 4 — "3–5 g/day saturates muscle stores; can't store any further" — Grade A**
Standard dosing. ISSN position stand: 3–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate sustains muscle creatine saturation indefinitely after a 3–4 week loading period; alternatively, a 5–7 day loading phase at 20 g/day can saturate faster. There is a true ceiling — supplementing beyond saturation provides no additional benefit.

**Claim 5 — "5–15% gains in strength and performance, supported by dozens of meta-analyses in men, women, young, old" — Grade A**
Matches the ISSN position stand. Meta-analyses (e.g., [Lanhers et al., *Sports Med* 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27328852/) for upper-body; [Lanhers et al., *Sports Med* 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25946994/) for lower-body) report 5–15% improvements in maximal strength and power across populations including older adults and women.

**Claim 6 — "Brain uses 20% of body's energy; creatine crosses blood-brain barrier" — Grade A**
True. The brain uses ~20% of basal metabolic rate despite being ~2% of body mass. Creatine crosses the blood-brain barrier via the SLC6A8 (creatine transporter) protein, though more slowly than into muscle — brain creatine pool augmentation typically requires longer or higher-dose supplementation.

**Claim 7 — "2024 systematic review: creatine improved cognitive performance under sleep deprivation and mental fatigue" — Grade B (real but with nuance)**
Recent literature does support this. The most-cited 2024 paper is [Gordji-Nejad et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54249-9) — a single high-dose (~25 g) of creatine improved cognitive performance and brain energy metabolism in sleep-deprived participants. Earlier work: [Roschel et al., *Nutrients* 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33670265/) review of creatine and cognition. The "sleep deprivation and mental fatigue" framing is the strongest current cognitive evidence; *general* cognitive benefits in well-rested adults are weaker. Krish's framing is appropriately scoped.

**Claim 8 — "Doesn't destroy kidneys — myth comes from creatinine measurement artifact" — Grade A (well-debunked)**
True and important. Long-term studies (5+ years of supplementation) show no clinically significant kidney or liver dysfunction in healthy adults. The persistent kidney myth comes from the fact that creatine is metabolized to creatinine, which is a serum marker doctors use to estimate glomerular filtration rate (GFR) — supplementation raises serum creatinine without indicating actual renal injury. Cystatin C-based GFR estimates show no change. ([Antonio et al., *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33557850/) — comprehensive safety review.) **Caveat Krish doesn't mention**: people with pre-existing kidney disease should still consult a nephrologist before supplementing.

**Claim 9 — "2 kg weight gain in a week is intracellular water, not bloating or fat" — Grade A**
True. Creatine retention is osmotically active and draws water into muscle cells. ~1–2 kg of water weight in the first 1–2 weeks is the textbook expectation. The intracellular hydration also supports protein synthesis as Krish notes.

**Claim 10 — "Vegetarians benefit most because they have lower baseline muscle creatine" — Grade A**
True and clinically meaningful in the Indian context. Vegetarians have ~30–50% lower muscle creatine stores than omnivores because they don't get the dietary 1–2 g/day. Supplementation produces *larger relative* gains in strength, lean mass, and cognitive performance in vegetarians vs. omnivores. Foundational study: [Burke et al., *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14600563/) — vegetarians supplementing creatine for 8 weeks showed significantly greater gains in lean mass and bench press 1RM than omnivores on the same regimen. The 2020 review he references is [Kaviani et al., *Nutrients* 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32872103/).

**Claim 11 — "Get monohydrate; fancy versions are marketing, no evidence" — Grade A**
True. Creatine monohydrate is the gold standard with the largest evidence base. Direct head-to-head trials of monohydrate vs. ethyl ester, HCl, buffered (Kre-Alkalyn), magnesium chelate, etc., have **not** demonstrated superiority of any of these over monohydrate in absorption, muscle saturation, or performance outcomes. Often these "advanced" forms are 5–10× more expensive with no incremental benefit. The ISSN position stand explicitly recommends monohydrate as the form of choice.

## Verdict
This is one of the cleanest creatine explainers on Indian-language social media. Every substantive claim is supported by current peer-reviewed evidence; the dosing recommendation (3–5 g/day monohydrate) matches the ISSN position stand; the kidney-myth debunk is accurate and addresses the most common Indian objection; the "vegetarians benefit most" framing is both true and unusually well-targeted to the Indian audience. ## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
Take it. Three to five grams a day of plain creatine monohydrate is one of the most bulletproof supplement decisions you can make — decades of studies, no real downsides at typical doses, modest but real strength gains, and increasingly real cognitive benefits especially when you're sleep-deprived. If you're vegetarian (which most of us are), the benefit is even bigger because your baseline muscle creatine is lower. Don't pay extra for the fancy "Kre-Alkalyn" or "creatine HCl" stuff — it's marketing, not better. The kidney scare is a measurement-artifact myth. The 2 kg you gain in week one is water in your muscles, not fat. Buy the cheapest reputable monohydrate and go.
