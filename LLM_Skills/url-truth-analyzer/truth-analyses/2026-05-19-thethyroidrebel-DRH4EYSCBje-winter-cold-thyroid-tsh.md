## Analysis
### Medical: SORT Analysis

The carousel makes four factual claims and one solution-list claim. I grade each separately, then give an overall grade.

**Claim 1 — TSH follows a circadian rhythm; highest at night, lowest mid-morning/early afternoon; therefore test at the same time of day.**
- Safety: benign clinical-pearl ("test at the same time"); no risk.
- Outcomes: DOE (test-result consistency), not a patient outcome — but the recommendation prevents misclassification, which downstream is patient-oriented.
- Risk of bias: physiology is established in multiple frequent-sampling studies (Russell et al 2008; Roelfsema & Veldhuis reviews).
- Total evidence: matches mainstream endocrinology and is reflected in laboratory-medicine guidance to standardise sample timing.
- **Grade A** for the physiology, **B** for the practical recommendation (DOE-flavoured).

**Claim 2 — TSH levels rise in winter as an adaptive response to colder temperatures and reduced daylight.**
- Safety: descriptive, no intervention.
- Outcomes: DOE (TSH is a surrogate for thyroid status, not a patient outcome).
- Risk of bias: the underlying evidence is observational (large cross-sectional cohorts and a 2021 meta-analysis), but findings are consistent across multiple independent datasets and geographies.
- Total evidence: consistent — Kuzmenko et al 2021 meta-analysis found TSH elevated in winter, especially in women; large cohort studies (Iranian, Korean, Italian) show the same direction; mechanism is partially debated (cold vs photoperiod) but the phenomenon is real and the shift is typically modest (~5–15% within-person variation, generally still within reference range).
- **Grade B** — consistent observational evidence with mechanistic plausibility but DOE outcome and no interventional RCT testing seasonal protocols.

**Claim 3 — This winter TSH rise causes slower metabolism, weight gain, and mood shifts.**
- Safety: framing harm — implies winter weight gain is a thyroid-driven, treatable problem rather than a multifactorial lifestyle phenomenon.
- Outcomes: this is the slide where she pivots from a DOE (TSH) to POEMs (weight, mood), which is the weakest link in the deck.
- Risk of bias: the causal step is not established. Modest seasonal TSH increases generally stay within the euthyroid range and have not been shown in interventional studies to materially drive winter weight gain or seasonal mood symptoms. Seasonal Affective Disorder is a real entity but is independently linked to photoperiod and serotonergic/circadian mechanisms, not principally to TSH.
- Total evidence: mainstream endocrinology does not attribute winter weight gain to seasonal TSH variation.
- **Grade C** — mechanistic extrapolation, not supported by outcome trials.

**Claim 4 — Lack of UVB in winter → low vitamin D → "an increase in autoimmune and systemic inflammation".**
- Safety: low; vitamin D supplementation at maintenance doses is safe.
- Outcomes: mixed. Vitamin D status is a DOE; antibody titres (TPO-Ab, Tg-Ab) are DOEs; clinical hypothyroid progression / cardiovascular events / symptom scores would be POEMs.
- Risk of bias: observational evidence linking low 25(OH)D to Hashimoto's is consistent (meta-analysis of 25 studies: OR ~3.2 for deficiency in HT cases vs controls). RCT evidence is weaker — multiple meta-analyses of small RCTs show vitamin D supplementation modestly lowers TPO-Ab over 3–6 months, but evidence that this translates into reduced hypothyroidism incidence, lower medication doses, or symptom improvement is limited.
- Total evidence: the vitamin-D-and-autoimmune-thyroid link is real but the "systemic inflammation" framing is broader than the evidence supports — vitamin D's effect on generic inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) in non-deficient populations is small and inconsistent.
- **Grade B** for the narrow vit-D-and-thyroid-antibodies link; **Grade C** for the broader "systemic inflammation" extrapolation.

**Claim 5 — The solution stack: align routines/sleep/light/food/movement to the season; tailored supplementation; red & IR light; brown fat activation; sauna; cold exposure.**
- Safety: cold-water immersion has documented cardiovascular risks (cold shock, arrhythmia in vulnerable people); sauna has cardiovascular contraindications; "tailored supplementation" without lab work is the usual nutritional-therapist failure mode. None of these are explicitly flagged in the slide.
- Outcomes: a grab-bag of biohacking interventions with very different evidence bases:
  - Circadian alignment / sleep / light exposure: Grade B for general metabolic and mood outcomes, indirect for thyroid.
  - Tailored vitamin D / selenium supplementation in deficient HT patients: Grade B (modest antibody effects).
  - Red / infrared light therapy on thyroid (LLLT): a small handful of studies, notably Höfling et al, suggest reduced thyroid medication needs in HT — but the evidence base is small, mostly single-centre, and not replicated at scale. Grade C.
  - Brown adipose tissue activation: live research area in obesity/metabolism, no clinical trials targeting hypothyroidism. Grade C.
  - Sauna: cardiovascular benefits in Finnish cohort studies (Laukkanen); not a thyroid intervention. Grade C for the thyroid framing.
  - Cold exposure: small studies on metabolism, immunity (Buijze et al); no thyroid-outcome RCTs. Grade C.
- **Grade C overall** for the solution stack as framed (a "thyroid winter protocol").

**Overall SORT grade: B for the descriptive physiology (Claims 1–2 + the narrow Claim 4); C for the causal extrapolation and intervention stack (Claims 3 + 5 + broad Claim 4).**

## Verdict
This is a well-produced wellness-coach carousel that mixes a layer of genuinely accurate endocrine physiology (diurnal and seasonal TSH variation) with a layer of much-shakier biohacking framing (sauna + cold exposure + red light + brown fat activation as a "thyroid winter protocol") in service of selling a £33 masterclass. The descriptive claims about TSH rhythms are SORT-B; the causal claim that winter TSH variation drives weight gain and mood shifts, and the suggested intervention stack, are SORT-C. Aggie McCabe stays on the more reasonable end of her niche — no thyroid-cure language, no supplement push, no extreme diet — but the slide that pivots from "TSH rises in winter" to "this is why you're gaining weight" is exactly the kind of mechanistic-to-clinical jump that gets people to buy programs for problems they may not have. Share the physiology slides if useful, take the protocol claims with skepticism, and do not let a £33 sticker price decide whether you need a clinical workup.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
The basic science here is real: your TSH (the hormone that tells your thyroid what to do) is a bit higher at night, a bit lower mid-morning, and tends to creep up a little in winter. That's why labs ask you to test at the same time of day. Where this post gets sketchy is the second half — it jumps from "TSH ticks up in winter" to "that's why you feel sluggish and gain weight, here's a £33 class to fix it." Most of that "winter protocol" (sauna, cold plunges, red light, brown fat hacks) is interesting research but not actual thyroid medicine. If you actually feel rough in winter and your weight is climbing, talk to a doctor and get a proper thyroid panel, vitamin D check, and a look at sleep and daylight — that gets you 90% of the benefit without the upsell.
