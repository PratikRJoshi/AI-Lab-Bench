## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four lenses

**Safety**
The recommendations (resistance training 2–3×/week, 1–1.2 g/kg/day protein, daily activity) are well within standard public-health bands and are essentially zero-harm for the general adult population. Standard caveats: (1) novice older adults restarting resistance training after a cardiac event should ideally have a tailored cardiac-rehab progression rather than self-prescribing — Dr. Pal post-MI obviously had clinician oversight, but a viewer extrapolating his post-MI protocol without clearance is a small but real safety gap the carousel doesn't flag; (2) the 1–1.2 g/kg protein target is safe even in moderate CKD (KDIGO recommends 0.6–0.8 g/kg in non-dialysis CKD stages 3b–5, so the post's blanket recommendation could be inappropriate for that subgroup); (3) "lift weights" without progression-of-load guidance can produce tendinopathy or rotator-cuff injury in untrained 50+ adults — but this is a normal limitation of a 9-slide format, not a misinformation issue.

**Outcomes (POEM vs DOE)**
The outcomes invoked are **patient-oriented (POEMs)**: weakness, falls, balance, "aging faster," and the implied morbidity/mortality consequences of sarcopenia. These are genuine, clinically meaningful endpoints — sarcopenia and low muscle mass are independently associated with all-cause mortality, falls, fracture risk, and disability in large cohort studies. The protein + resistance training recommendations have RCT evidence specifically for **lean-mass preservation during weight loss** (a DOE-leaning surrogate of muscle quantity, but with downstream POEM linkage to function), and separately for **functional outcomes** (gait speed, chair-rise, fall incidence) in older adults. So this content is patient-oriented in framing and largely backed by patient-oriented evidence.

**Risk of bias**
The carousel cites no specific studies, but the underlying recommendations (resistance training + ≈1.0–1.6 g/kg protein during weight loss in older adults) are mature consensus across multiple independent guideline bodies (ESPEN, PROT-AGE, ACSM, WHO Physical Activity Guidelines), so risk of bias from the *literature* is low. The framing risk of bias is the simplification: the cited decadal rates ("3–5% in 30s–40s, 5–10% after 50") are taken from a wide literature range and presented without confidence intervals. In reality, the most-cited estimates are closer to ~0.5–1%/year in healthy adults from age 30, accelerating to 1–2%/year after 60, with very large inter-individual variation depending on activity, hormonal status, illness, and diet. Dr. Pal's numbers are not wrong, but they are at the high-attention end of the range and are presented as if they were universal. Commercial bias: NewME is the structural conflict, but this specific post does not pitch a NewME product, plan, or coaching service — it's content marketing for the broader brand rather than a direct sale.

**Total evidence**
The core message — *"resistance training plus higher dietary protein during weight loss preserves lean mass better than diet alone"* — is one of the better-established findings in clinical nutrition / exercise science:
- Multiple RCTs (Longland 2016, Mettler 2010, Pasiakos 2013, Layman 2009) show higher-protein diets during caloric restriction preserve lean mass and lose more fat mass vs. lower-protein controls.
- Cava et al. 2017 narrative review and the 2014 PROT-AGE consensus recommend ≥1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults and **1.2–1.5 g/kg/day or higher** during weight loss, illness, or for active older adults — already higher than what Dr. Pal recommends.
- The 2020 USDA / HHS Dietary Guidelines and ESPEN 2014 reach the same conclusion.
- For sarcopenia and functional outcomes, the evidence base is mature: Cruz-Jentoft EWGSOP2 consensus, Liu & Latham Cochrane 2009 (resistance training improves physical functioning in older adults — Grade A POEM), and large prospective cohorts on muscle mass and mortality.

So the recommendation direction (lift + protein) is on solid Grade A ground; the **specific dose** is conservative-to-low; the decadal rates are headline-simplified.

#### Pass 2 — SORT grade

**Overall: Grade B.**

This is a Grade B because:
- The qualitative recommendations (resistance training to preserve lean mass during weight loss; adequate dietary protein during weight loss in adults; resistance training for sarcopenia prevention) have **Grade A** patient-oriented RCT and meta-analytic evidence.
- However, the **specific** quantitative claims in the post — "1–1.2 g/kg protein" as a universal weight-loss target, and the decadal percentage ranges — represent a simplification of a wider literature, not a precise reproduction of any specific guideline. Modern weight-loss / older-adult guidance generally targets **1.2–1.6+ g/kg/day**, especially during caloric deficit. So the slide-by-slide claims are limited-quality on the numbers while being well-supported on the direction.
- No retractions, no contradiction with mainstream literature, no commercial product pitch in this specific reel.

A viewer who took the qualitative message at face value ("when losing weight, lift and eat enough protein so you don't lose muscle") would be acting on Grade A advice. A viewer who treated "1–1.2 g/kg" as the actual target during a weight-loss phase would likely under-eat protein per modern consensus.

## Verdict
**Largely trustworthy with one notable underspecification.** The carousel's qualitative thesis — *"when you lose weight, you can lose muscle alongside fat; resistance training plus adequate dietary protein protects against this; muscle loss accelerates with age and matters for function, not just appearance"* — is essentially mainstream geriatric / clinical-nutrition consensus, backed by Grade A RCT and meta-analytic evidence. The recommendations to lift 2–3×/week and stay active daily are aligned with WHO and ACSM physical activity guidelines. The single number to push back on is the protein target: **1–1.2 g/kg/day is the lower bound of healthy older-adult intake; for someone in active weight loss (which is the post's literal scenario), modern guidelines (PROT-AGE, ESPEN, Cava 2017) recommend ~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day or higher.** Followers who set 1.2 g/kg as a ceiling rather than a floor during a cut will likely fall short. Decadal muscle-loss rates are presented at the high-attention end of a wide literature range — directionally correct, numerically simplified. SORT grade: **B**, with the directional message at Grade A and the numeric specifics at Grade B–C.
