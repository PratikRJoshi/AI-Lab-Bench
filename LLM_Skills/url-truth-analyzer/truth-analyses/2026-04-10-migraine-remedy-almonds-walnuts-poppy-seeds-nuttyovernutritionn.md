# Truth Analysis: Migraine Remedy — Almonds, Walnuts & Poppy Seeds Smoothie
**Source URL**: https://www.instagram.com/p/C2unE5OyYFK/
**Analyzed**: 2026-04-10
**Content type**: Medical
**Format**: Video (35 seconds)
**Creator**: Kiran Kukreja (@nuttyovernutritionn) — Nutritionist / Weight Management / Skin & Hair

## Summary

A nutritionist presents a case of an 18-year-old student experiencing 4–5 migraine attacks per week. Root cause analysis reportedly revealed the student was skipping breakfast daily, leading to hypoglycemia. The remedy recommended: soak 5 almonds, 2 walnuts, and 1 spoon of poppy seeds for 4–5 hours, then grind with 2 pitted dates and 150–200 ml of cow's milk. The smoothie is recommended once daily (preferably evening). The claim is that these ingredients are rich in magnesium, zinc, calcium, and potassium — minerals that help "cure" migraines.

## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Analysis Lenses

##### 1. Safety Lens

- **No absolute risk figures** are provided — no mention of how many migraine patients respond to dietary changes vs. those who need pharmacological treatment.
- **No contraindications discussed**: Poppy seeds (khus khus) contain trace opioid alkaloids (morphine, codeine, thebaine). While food-grade amounts are generally safe, they can cause false positives on drug tests, and in rare cases, hypersensitivity reactions. This is not mentioned.
- **No side effects mentioned**: Magnesium from dietary sources is generally safe, but the post does not distinguish between food-sourced minerals and supplemental doses.
- **The word "cure" is used**: This is a strong claim. Migraines are a complex neurological condition with no single cure. The language implies definitive resolution, not management.
- **No mention of when to seek medical care**: For a patient with 4–5 attacks/week, neurological evaluation and prophylactic medication (beta-blockers, anticonvulsants, CGRP inhibitors) should be considered. Relying solely on a smoothie remedy risks delayed treatment.

##### 2. Outcomes Lens

| Claimed Outcome | Classification | Evidence Level |
|---|---|---|
| Remedy "cures" migraines | POEM (symptom relief) | No clinical trial cited — anecdotal case report |
| Magnesium helps reduce migraines | DOE→POEM bridge | Supported by RCTs (500–600 mg supplemental, not food-sourced) |
| Skipping breakfast → hypoglycemia → migraines | POEM (trigger identification) | Supported by observational studies |
| Zinc, calcium, potassium cure migraines | DOE (mineral status) | Limited/mixed evidence; observational associations only |

**Critical issue**: The magnesium content of a smoothie with 5 almonds (~14 mg Mg each = ~70 mg) + 2 walnuts (~16 mg each = ~32 mg) + 1 tbsp poppy seeds (~30 mg) ≈ **~130 mg magnesium**. Clinical trials showing migraine benefit use **500–600 mg/day** of supplemental magnesium — roughly 4–5× more than this smoothie provides. The remedy delivers a fraction of the therapeutic dose studied in trials.

##### 3. Risk of Bias Lens

- **Study design**: This is a **single case report/anecdote** (Level 7 on the evidence hierarchy) — one unnamed 18-year-old student. No control group, no blinding, no follow-up data reported.
- **Creator conflict of interest**: The creator is a nutritionist promoting natural/dietary remedies as a business. The "21 Days 21 Tips for Natural Healing" framing is content-marketing-oriented.
- **Cherry-picking**: Only one "success story" is presented. No mention of patients for whom this approach did not work.
- **Causal attribution fallacy**: The student was also given "lifestyle changes" (mentioned in passing). The migraine improvement could be from regular meals/breakfast resumption, not the smoothie itself. The remedy and the lifestyle change are confounded.
- **No peer-reviewed citation**: No study or clinical guideline is referenced.

##### 4. Total Evidence Lens

- **Magnesium for migraines**: Well-supported by RCTs and systematic reviews, but at supplemental doses (500–600 mg/day), not dietary amounts from a single smoothie. A 2024 meta-analysis of 22 RCTs confirmed magnesium reduces migraine attack frequency. The gap between evidence-based dosing and this smoothie's magnesium content is large.
- **Skipping meals as a migraine trigger**: A 2025 scoping review of 36 studies supports this link through hypoglycemia, though methodological quality varies. This is the strongest evidence-based claim in the video.
- **Poppy seeds for migraines**: No clinical evidence exists. Traditional use does not constitute clinical evidence.
- **Zinc, calcium, potassium for migraines**: Cross-sectional NHANES analyses show associations between dietary zinc/calcium intake and lower migraine prevalence, but these are observational and cannot establish causation. No RCT evidence for these minerals as migraine treatments.
- **Clinical guidelines**: The American Headache Society and American Academy of Neurology include magnesium supplementation (400–600 mg/day) as a Level B recommendation for migraine prophylaxis — but as a supplement, not via a smoothie delivering ~130 mg.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade Assignment

### **SORT Grade: C**

**Rationale**: The recommendation rests on a single anecdotal case report (the unnamed student) with no clinical trial data, no control group, and mechanistic reasoning only ("these nutrients help migraines"). While the underlying science about magnesium and migraines is Grade A–B level, the specific claim that *this particular smoothie at this dose cures migraines* has no direct evidence. The use of the word "cure" goes well beyond what even the best magnesium studies show (which demonstrate reduction in frequency, not cure). The confounding of lifestyle changes with the remedy further weakens the causal claim.

## Verdict

This video makes a medically misleading claim. While the general principles underlying the advice — regular meals prevent hypoglycemia-triggered migraines, and magnesium plays a role in migraine prophylaxis — are supported by real evidence, the specific remedy is problematic. The smoothie delivers roughly 130 mg of magnesium, well below the 500–600 mg therapeutic dose used in clinical trials. The claim that this recipe "cures" migraines is an overstatement unsupported by any clinical evidence. Most importantly, for a patient experiencing 4–5 migraine attacks per week, professional neurological evaluation and evidence-based prophylactic treatment should be the first-line approach, not an Instagram smoothie recipe. The single-case anecdote format, confounding with lifestyle changes, and the "natural healing" marketing frame further reduce credibility. **Grade C — treat with significant skepticism.**
