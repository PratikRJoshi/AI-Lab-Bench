#### Pass 1 — Four lenses

- **Safety**: Zero discussion of risks. The narrative implicitly invites viewers off levothyroxine ("there was no conventional treatment"). Unsupervised tapering of thyroid hormone in Hashimoto's hypothyroidism can cause severe symptomatic hypothyroidism, myxedema, pregnancy complications, and cardiovascular effects. "Functional labs" (e.g., DUTCH, GI-MAP, food-sensitivity IgG panels) marketed by coaching businesses are largely unvalidated for autoimmune-thyroid management and can generate expensive false-positive cascades. No NNT, NNH, or any absolute risk/benefit numbers appear. No mention that TPO antibodies fluctuate naturally and do not define clinical remission.
- **Outcomes (POEM vs DOE)**: The headline outcome — "Hashimoto's went into remission" — is a DOE wearing POEM clothing. Hashimoto's "remission" is not a standard endocrinology endpoint; what the post almost certainly means is "TPO antibodies dropped," which is a surrogate marker that has not been shown to track with thyroid function, symptoms, or long-term outcomes. The 50 lb weight loss and "symptom improvement" are POEM-relevant but reported only as n=1 self-report plus two anonymous client testimonials with no baseline metrics, no follow-up duration beyond the photo, and no comparator group.
- **Risk of bias**: Maximum. (1) Creator is the seller of the intervention. (2) Anecdotal evidence only (single self-case + cherry-picked client photos). (3) Survivorship/selection bias in testimonials. (4) No blinding, no controls, no protocol pre-registration, no published data. (5) The "Functional Nutrition" framing is itself an unregulated descriptor.
- **Total evidence**: Contradicts the "no conventional treatment" claim — levothyroxine remains the consensus standard of care for the hypothyroidism that Hashimoto's commonly causes, supported by ATA and Endocrine Society guidelines [1]. Evidence for diet/gut interventions in Hashimoto's exists but is small and mixed: a small RCT of a gluten-free diet showed modest TPO/TgAb reductions in drug-naïve euthyroid Hashimoto's women [2]; selenium meta-analyses show small antibody-titer reductions of uncertain clinical relevance [3]; a single open-label AIP-diet pilot reported quality-of-life and inflammatory-marker improvements without antibody or thyroid-function changes [4]; broader reviews emphasize adjunctive role at best [5]. None of this literature supports "remission" as a programmatic outcome, and none validates direct-to-consumer functional-lab-driven protocols.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade

**Grade C** — The post's core therapeutic claims rest on personal anecdote, cherry-picked testimonials, and mechanistic reasoning ("root cause," "gut protocol"). There are no RCTs, no systematic reviews, and no patient-oriented endpoints with controls supporting "Hashimoto's remission" from this specific intervention. The flat statement that "there is no conventional treatment for Hashimoto's" is **misleading**: it equivocates between "no immune-modulating cure" (true in a narrow sense) and "no treatment exists" (false — levothyroxine is the established treatment for the resulting hypothyroidism and has Grade A evidence behind it).

## Verdict
This is a well-crafted but evidence-weak Hashimoto's marketing carousel. The personal story is plausible and probably true; the *generalizable medical claims* layered on top — that conventional medicine has nothing for Hashimoto's, that a "gut protocol" caused autoimmune remission, that direct-to-consumer functional labs identify and resolve "root causes," and that two before/after photos prove a program works — are unsupported by controlled evidence and contradicted by current guidelines on hypothyroidism management. Treat the post as an advertisement, not as health information. SORT Grade C.

Hey — this is a sales post dressed up as a Hashimoto's story. Her experience may be real, but the big claims ("no real treatment exists," "my gut protocol cured my Hashimoto's," "look at these two before/afters") aren't backed by actual studies, and the standard thyroid-medication treatment she dismisses is still the one with the strongest evidence. The "functional labs" she sells aren't validated for Hashimoto's either. Bottom line: don't change your thyroid meds, diet, or labs based on this — talk to an endocrinologist or RD/CNS instead.
