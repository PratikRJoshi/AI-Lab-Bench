## Analysis

### Medical: SORT Analysis

**Core claims extracted from slides 1, 2, 4, 6–14:**
1. People with chronic conditions (knee pain, back pain, stiffness, obesity, old injuries) should not start exercising the same way as the general population.
2. The "wrong" workout can worsen an existing condition, and many people then quit exercising altogether.
3. The problem is not movement itself — it is doing movements the body is not yet ready for.
4. Some people need mobility work, joint-friendly exercises, posture correction, and muscle strengthening before intense workouts.
5. A physiotherapist evaluation can identify joint stress, pain triggers, movement limitations, prior-injury concerns, and exercises to avoid, so the program can be adjusted.
6. Exercise should be designed around the individual's body for safe, consistent, sustainable progress.

#### Pass 1 — Four lenses

- **Safety**: The post's safety framing is conservative and reasonable. It does not promise dramatic outcomes, does not push a specific drug or supplement, and does not discourage care. The risk of harm from following the carousel's advice (get evaluated, start gently, progress gradually) is very low. The implicit harm — paying for a program one may not need — is the main downside, not a clinical risk.
- **Outcomes (POEM vs DOE)**: The strongest claims (tailored exercise improves pain, function, and quality of life in chronic low-back pain, knee osteoarthritis, and cardiovascular disease) are backed by high-quality **POEM** evidence — Cochrane reviews and clinical guidelines report patient-oriented benefits (less pain, better function, lower mortality in cardiac rehab). The weaker claim — that universal pre-exercise screening prevents injury in healthy or low-risk adults — is supported mostly by **expert opinion** and risk-stratification frameworks (PAR-Q+, ACSM 2015), not by RCTs showing reduced injury rates from screening itself.
- **Risks of bias**: The biggest issue is **commercial conflict of interest** — the post sells NewME consultations. There is no cited literature, no quantification ("can sometimes worsen the problem", "many people"), and the carousel uses anecdotal framing ("hundreds of messages"). The author is a gastroenterologist, not a sports-medicine physician or physiotherapist — so the specific physiotherapy claims are outside his sub-specialty even though they are mainstream.
- **Total evidence**: The general thesis is consistent with the broader literature. ACSM 2015 pre-participation screening guidance, AHA exercise prescription statements, OARSI knee-OA guidelines, and Cochrane reviews of exercise for chronic LBP, knee OA, and cardiac rehab all converge on the same idea: tailored, professionally guided exercise is safe and beneficial in chronic conditions, and people with red-flag features (cardiac symptoms, severe pain, recent injury) should be evaluated before starting moderate-vigorous training. Nothing in the post is an outlier.

#### Pass 2 — SORT grade

- For the message *"tailored, professionally guided exercise improves outcomes in people with chronic MSK conditions and CVD"* → **Grade A** (consistent POEM evidence from systematic reviews and RCTs).
- For the message *"pre-exercise screening prevents injuries / worsening of conditions in the general population"* → **Grade C** (largely expert opinion and consensus frameworks; little RCT evidence that screening itself reduces injury rates in low-risk asymptomatic adults).
- **Overall post grade: Grade B.** The clinical advice is broadly correct and consistent with mainstream guidelines, the framing is conservative on safety, but specific quantitative claims are absent, the commercial conflict is large, and the injury-prevention claim is weaker than the carousel's confident tone implies.

## Verdict
The clinical core of this post is sound and aligned with mainstream guidelines: people with chronic pain, prior injury, obesity, or chronic disease genuinely do benefit from a tailored, professionally guided exercise plan, and a physiotherapist or sports-medicine clinician is a reasonable starting point for that. There is no medical misinformation in the carousel, and the safety framing is conservative. The grade is held at B (rather than A) because the injury-prevention claim is stated more confidently than the evidence supports for the general population, no quantification or citations are provided, and the entire deck is functionally an ad for the author's paid NewME program — a large, undisclosed-in-context commercial conflict of interest. Trust the underlying advice; do not treat NewME as the only or best path to it.
