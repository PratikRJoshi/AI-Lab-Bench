# Truth Analysis: Do Not Make Your Bed — Dust Mites and Your Health
**Source URL**: https://www.instagram.com/p/C2kVfKyhrz9/
**Analyzed**: 2026-04-10
**Content type**: Medical
**Format**: Video (short-form Instagram Reel, ~61 seconds)

## Summary
This Instagram video claims you should not make your bed immediately upon waking because trapping moisture from overnight sweat creates an ideal environment for dust mites, which produce allergens that trigger asthma and nasal congestion. It further claims an average mattress harbors over 10 million dust mites and that a two-year-old pillow consists of 10% dust mite weight (mites + feces). The video recommends leaving the bed unmade for 1–2 hours and washing sheets/pillowcases weekly (or at least biweekly).

## Analysis

### EBM SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Analysis Lenses

**1. Safety**
- No pharmaceutical intervention is discussed, so no drug side effects or contraindications apply.
- The video addresses a real safety concern: house dust mite (HDM) allergens are a well-established trigger for allergic rhinitis and asthma. The video correctly identifies that HDM fecal pellets contain Der p 1 and Der f 1 proteins, which are potent aeroallergens.
- No absolute risk numbers are provided (e.g., what percentage of people are sensitized to HDM allergens, or how much leaving a bed unmade actually reduces mite counts). The video relies on emotional framing ("they're having a party, procreating") rather than quantified risk.
- **Omission**: The video does not mention that HDM allergen avoidance through bed-making changes alone has shown mixed results in clinical trials. A major Cochrane review found no significant improvement in asthma symptoms from physical/chemical HDM avoidance measures including mattress covers.

**2. Outcomes**
- **Claim 1** (leaving bed unmade reduces dust mites): This is a **disease-oriented endpoint (DOE)**. Reducing mite counts is a surrogate marker — it does not directly measure whether patients experienced fewer asthma attacks, less nasal congestion, or improved quality of life.
- **Claim 2** (dust mites trigger asthma/congestion): This is supported by patient-oriented evidence (POEMs) — HDM sensitization is consistently associated with asthma exacerbations in epidemiological studies and immunotherapy trials.
- **Claim 3** (washing sheets weekly helps): This is practical hygiene advice supported by expert consensus and allergen reduction studies, but no RCT specifically tests "weekly vs. biweekly sheet washing" as an asthma outcome measure.
- The core recommendation (leave bed unmade) rests on DOE only — no clinical trial has measured patient outcomes from this specific behavioral change.

**3. Risk of Bias**
- The Kingston University study (Pretlove et al., 2005) used a **computer simulation model** to predict hygrothermal conditions in beds and their effect on mite populations. This is not an experimental study — it modeled predicted outcomes based on environmental parameters. No actual mite counting or patient outcomes were measured.
- The "10 million dust mites" figure is widely cited but acknowledged by researchers (including Ohio State entomologist Emmett Glass) as lacking rigorous documentation. The actual range is 100,000 to 10 million depending on conditions, with the higher figure representing worst-case estimates used commercially.
- The "10% pillow weight" claim is a popular factoid with uncertain provenance. While some sources cite it, the Ohio State researcher who was frequently misquoted clarified these statistics "are not in the literature."
- The video says "this is according to science" without citing any specific study, creating an appeal to authority without verifiable evidence.

**4. Total Evidence**
- **Dust mites cause allergic disease**: Well-established, Grade A–level evidence from decades of immunological research, epidemiological studies, and successful immunotherapy trials.
- **Leaving the bed unmade kills dust mites**: Based on a single computational modeling study (Kingston University, 2005). Not replicated by experimental studies measuring actual mite populations in made vs. unmade beds under real-world conditions.
- **HDM avoidance reduces asthma symptoms**: A Cochrane systematic review of 55 RCTs (Gøtzsche & Johansen, 2008; updated 2012) involving 3,121 patients found **no significant benefit** of physical or chemical HDM reduction methods on asthma outcomes. However, a subsequent high-quality RCT in children showed mite-impermeable encasings reduced emergency hospital visits for acute asthma.
- **Sheet washing frequency**: Expert consensus (American Academy of Dermatology, AAFA) recommends weekly washing at ≥130°F/54°C. This is Grade C evidence (consensus-based) for symptom reduction.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade

**Overall SORT Grade: C**

Rationale: The central recommendation ("don't make your bed") rests on a single computer-modeling study with no clinical outcome data. While the underlying biology of dust mites as allergens is well-established (Grade A for the association), the specific behavioral intervention proposed has never been tested in a clinical trial measuring patient-oriented outcomes. The numerical claims (10 million mites, 10% pillow weight) are either upper-bound estimates or poorly documented factoids. The Cochrane review on HDM avoidance strategies found no significant asthma benefit, which undermines the video's implication that this simple habit change meaningfully improves health.

## Verdict
The video contains a kernel of biological truth wrapped in exaggeration and unsupported specifics. Yes, dust mites are real allergens that thrive in moist bedding, and airing out a bed can theoretically reduce moisture — but this recommendation comes from a single computer-modeling study, not from any clinical trial showing health benefits. The specific numbers cited (10 million mites, 10% pillow weight) are popular but poorly documented factoids. Most importantly, the Cochrane Collaboration's review of 55 randomized controlled trials found that physical dust mite avoidance measures do not significantly improve asthma outcomes. The sheet-washing advice (weekly, hot water) is sound practical hygiene supported by expert consensus. Overall, this is a social media health tip that oversells a plausible-but-unproven behavioral change as if it were settled science. **SORT Grade C — consensus/mechanistic reasoning only, no patient-oriented clinical evidence for the specific intervention recommended.**
