# Truth Analysis: Joe Rogan Experience #2502 — David Paulides

**Source URL**: https://youtu.be/_UduwgMDR-0
**Analyzed**: 2026-05-24
**Content type**: General Science (paranormal / cryptozoology / conspiracy framing; testable claims about NPS missing-persons data, canine-search behavior, Bigfoot DNA, abduction case files, and historical UFO incidents)
**Format**: Podcast (long), 15 chunks (~10-min intervals) via YouTube auto-captions, runtime ~2h 20m (8,425 s).

**Share?**: No — entertainment only, with explicit warnings. The episode is two friendly conversationalists riffing on Paulides's "Missing 411" framework, Bigfoot, alien abduction, and DMT cosmology, with almost no probing of the underlying methodology. The empirically testable claims that are made — disappearance "clusters" tied to water/granite/weather, canine universal failure, the Ketchum Bigfoot DNA "study," the Carl Higdon abduction, Kenneth Arnold and the Marine transport, Gigantopithecus bipedalism — are predominantly **refuted**, **contested**, or **unverified anecdotes**. None of the harm here is direct (no dietary or medical instruction), but the cumulative effect is the same epistemic damage Paulides has been critiqued for since ~2013: assembling vivid, individually-unsourceable cases into a "pattern" that is never tested against base rates or alternative explanations. Share for entertainment context only; do not forward as evidence of anything.

## Summary
Joe Rogan hosts David Paulides — former San Jose PD officer, founder of CanAm Missing Project, and author of the self-published *Missing 411* book series — for a wide-ranging conversation across (a) Paulides's signature claim that the National Park Service obstructs missing-persons data, (b) a small catalogue of disappearance cases he says defy conventional explanation (Stacy Arras, Gilbert Gilman, the Toronto firefighter, "Reza," Mt. Rainier hikers), (c) Carl Higdon's 1974 Wyoming abduction story, (d) the Travis Walton case, (e) the Melba Ketchum Sasquatch DNA paper, (f) Native American narratives that Bigfoot is "from the stars," and (g) digressions into Skinwalker Ranch, Amanita muscaria/Santa Claus, DMT, ayahuasca, the Yunnan "little people" mushroom, and the *Age of Disclosure* film. Rogan offers minimal pushback; the few moments of skepticism come from Jamie's live web lookups (Perplexity), which Paulides repeatedly waves off.

## Channel Reputation
**Host**: Joe Rogan / PowerfulJRE. ~14M+ active listeners across YouTube and Spotify; long-form (2–3 h) interview format. Documented mixed factual record across the show's run — strong on guests with empirical specialties (e.g., paleontology, evolutionary biology), substantially weaker on paranormal/conspiracy guests where Rogan's stated "I'm willing to entertain it" stance functionally yields the floor. Rogan typically introduces caveats ("putting the tinfoil hat on") but rarely follows up with adversarial questions or counter-evidence. Jamie (Young Jamie) acts as a real-time fact-checker, but his role on this episode is mostly retrieval, not adjudication — when Perplexity returns a debunking summary of the Ketchum Sasquatch genome paper (Chunk 7), Paulides simply asserts the AI is "talking to a bunch of idiots" and the conversation moves on.

**Guest**: David Paulides. Credentials he asserts on the episode (former San Jose PD officer, master's in HR, technology-industry HR roles) are consistent with the public record. He left law enforcement in the late 1990s and turned to Bigfoot/disappearance research full-time c. 2008; founded CanAm Missing Project in 2011 and has self-published ~10 books in the *Missing 411* series plus produced five documentaries (*Missing 411: The Movie*, *The Hunted*, *The UFO Connection*, *National Parks: Washington State*, *American Sasquatch: Man, Myth or Monster*). His core empirical claim — that disappearances in U.S. national parks cluster non-randomly around boulder fields, water, granite, and weather changes, and that canines fail in these cases at anomalous rates — has been **publicly and repeatedly critiqued**:

- **Brian Dunning (Skeptoid, 2014, "Missing 411" episode #427)** examined ~50 of Paulides's named cases against the conventional missing-persons literature and concluded the "411 profile" is selection bias: Paulides excludes cases with known animal predation, criminal involvement, or obvious mental-health crises, then is left with the residual "unexplained" cases and treats that residual as a pattern.
- **Robert Sheaffer (Bad UFOs blog, 2017 and 2019)** documented Paulides's case-by-case overstatement (e.g., described canine "failures" that on examination are normal short-trail confusion, weather-driven scent loss, or cases where dogs were not deployed under recoverable conditions).
- **Kyle Polich's "Data Skeptic" / community deep-dives (2018–2022)** noted that Paulides has never produced a denominator: how many people go missing in U.S. national parks per year, by park, controlled for visitation? The NPS's own data (compiled post-2018 after Congressional inquiry) shows the disappearance rate is on the order of 13–25 per 100M visits — comparable to or lower than equivalent rural-county base rates.
- **No peer-reviewed publication.** Paulides has not published his statistical methodology or his "411 criteria" in any controlled, peer-reviewed venue. His DNA collaboration with Dr. Melba Ketchum (discussed at length in Chunks 6–7) was published in *DeNovo Scientific Journal*, an outlet Ketchum effectively controlled to publish her own paper after rejection elsewhere — not a peer-reviewed channel.

**Net**: a credible policing background and a strong storytelling instinct, mated to a methodology that the relevant skeptical literature has repeatedly characterized as cherry-picking, base-rate-neglect, and anecdote aggregation. Treat the empirical claims accordingly.

## Analysis — Claim Validation

Claims are quoted (light cleanup of auto-caption artifacts), tagged with the chunk and approximate minute mark, and graded **SUPPORTED / CONTESTED / REFUTED / UNVERIFIED** with the mechanism.

### Chunk 1 (~0 min) — Genesis story; first wave of claims

**Claim 1.1** — Two Yosemite rangers approached Paulides in his park lodging and told him NPS had blocked their own internal FOIA requests on missing-persons reports.
- **Status**: **UNVERIFIED.** Single-source anecdote; the rangers are never named. NPS employees can and do file internal records requests through HR or the agency FOIA officer, but a personally directed FOIA on operational case files would normally be denied on standard 7(A) / 6 / 7(C) grounds while a case is technically open, regardless of intent. The "rangers couldn't get the reports" framing implies suppression where ordinary FOIA exemption practice is sufficient explanation.

**Claim 1.2** — A Toronto firefighter skiing in New York went missing and woke up days later in the front seat of a truck heading from Reno to Sacramento, in full ski clothes, with no memory.
- **Status**: **UNVERIFIED ANECDOTE.** No name given, no date, no police report cited, no contemporaneous news reference. The story has the hallmarks of a Paulides "amnesia/teleport" archetype and is structurally indistinguishable from the unsourced cases catalogued in *The Hunted* and *The UFO Connection*.

**Claim 1.3** — "I have 1,500 of those cases where canines were brought in, multiple K9 teams, multiple searches, and people were not found … I don't believe they were there when they were searching. They were left there later on."
- **Status**: **REFUTED** as a generalization. Search-and-rescue literature (NASAR, Koester's *Lost Person Behavior*, 2008; Cooper, Frost & Robe 2003) documents that K9 trailing dogs have a **published success rate of roughly 35–60%** under operational conditions, with sharp degradation in (a) hot dry conditions, (b) >24 h scent age, (c) running water, (d) high-traffic trails, (e) granitic/rocky substrate. All five of those degraders are systematically over-represented in Paulides's case set — because Paulides selects for "weird" cases, which are by definition cases where the dogs failed. The mechanism is **base-rate neglect compounded by selection bias**: the absolute count of dog failures sounds high but the appropriate comparator (canine success rate on demographically/topographically matched lost-person cases) is not provided.

### Chunk 2 (~10 min) — Stacy Arras, FOIA denials, NPS missing-persons list

**Claim 2.1** — Stacy Arras, 14, disappeared at Sunrise High Sierra Camp in Yosemite in 1981; her camera lens cap was found and nothing else; FOIA on the case has been denied for 40+ years under exemption 7(A).
- **Status**: **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED, MISFRAMED.** The disappearance is real (July 17, 1981) and the case is genuinely unsolved. However, FOIA exemption 7(A) does not require an investigation to be *active* — it requires the records to be compiled for law-enforcement purposes such that release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Once a missing-persons case has no reasonable prospect of further enforcement action, 7(A) generally cannot be sustained — but 7(C) (personal privacy of third parties named in the file) and 6 (privacy of living relatives' information) routinely justify continued withholding indefinitely. Paulides's framing ("they're calling it ongoing 40 years later, that's weird") collapses these distinct exemptions and converts standard FOIA administrative behavior into evidence of conspiracy.

**Claim 2.2** — NPS told Paulides it would cost $1.4M to produce a system-wide missing-persons list, $34K for Yosemite alone; that no such list existed; that NPS subsequently released a Yosemite list because of his pressure.
- **Status**: **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** It is well-documented (GAO-18-72, *National Park Service: Information on the Number, Cost, and Length of Stay of Missing Persons Cases*, 2018) that prior to 2018 NPS did **not** maintain a centralized, queryable database of missing-persons cases; cases were tracked at the unit/park level. So Paulides's "they said they don't have a list" is **factually correct** for the relevant period. The $1.4M / $34K cost estimates are plausible FOIA cost-recovery figures for unconsolidated paper records spread across ~400 NPS units; they are not evidence of suppression, they are evidence of decentralized recordkeeping. NPS has, since 2018, posted aggregated missing-persons data publicly in response to GAO and Congressional pressure — Tim Burchett (R-TN, mentioned on the episode) has been one of those Congressional pressure points. The conspiratorial framing is unnecessary; the bureaucratic framing fits the same facts.

### Chunk 3 (~20 min) — The "411 criteria" and the Carl Higdon case

**Claim 3.1** — Paulides's "Missing 411 criteria": point of separation, canine failure, professional tracker failure, weather change, proximity to water, proximity to granite/boulders, missing clothing.
- **Status**: **CONTESTED / METHODOLOGICALLY UNSOUND.** Each individual feature is *common* in lost-person cases: point of separation is the modal moment for someone to go missing in a hiking group regardless of cause; canine and tracker failure are common as discussed above; weather change is associated with disappearances because most disappearances happen during outdoor activity, which is correlated with weather; proximity to water, granite, and boulder fields is a near-tautological feature of the national parks where these cases occur. The "criteria" do not constitute a falsifiable model — they describe the dataset they were extracted from. Skeptoid #427 and the *Lost Person Behavior* literature note that you can apply the "411 profile" to any large sample of wilderness disappearances and recover an apparent pattern that disappears under controlled comparison.

**Claim 3.2** — Carl Higdon, an oil driller from Wyoming, was abducted in 1974 from the Medicine Bow National Forest; his bullet stopped mid-air and dropped to the ground; he was taken to an alien planet "163,000 light years away"; tuberculosis scars he'd had since childhood were healed.
- **Status**: **CONTESTED — UFOlogy case, not science.** This is a real case in the UFO literature (the Higdon abduction has been written about since the mid-70s, including by Robert Sheaffer in *Psychology Today* 1978). It is not, however, "investigated by the Wyoming Department of Law Enforcement" in any sense that produced a forensic finding of the kind Paulides implies. The bullet deformation photograph in *The Hunted* has no chain of custody documented in any independent forensic source. Higdon's account is one of dozens of similar 1970s abduction narratives that share their structure (mind-speak, frozen animals, medical examination, healing of pre-existing conditions, return to a different location, missing time) — that structural similarity is the relevant *psychological* finding (cf. Susan Clancy, *Abducted*, Harvard UP 2005), not a physical one. The TB-scar-removal claim is unverifiable post hoc and depends entirely on a 91-year-old Higdon's recollection ~40 years after the fact.

**Claim 3.3** — At the same time as Higdon's encounter, Cheyenne Air Force Base ICBM sites had a cluster of UFO sightings, later revealed via Robert Hastings's research.
- **Status**: **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** Hastings's *UFOs and Nukes* (2008, updated 2017) compiles veteran testimony of UFO incidents at U.S. nuclear sites including F.E. Warren AFB / Quebec Flight (Cheyenne, March 1967, Salas/Mansmann affidavits) and Malmstrom AFB. The testimony is real; whether it constitutes evidence of *non-human* craft is not established by any official record. Hastings's correlation with Higdon (1974) is geographically and temporally loose — Higdon was ~75 mi NW of Cheyenne, the documented Cheyenne ICBM incidents most commonly cited are from 1967 and 1975, not 1974.

**Claim 3.4** — "There's a lot of German people that have been abducted more than the normal population of people."
- **Status**: **UNSUPPORTED.** No base-rate analysis, no denominator, no source. Paulides himself says "I really wish I knew. That's probably outside my pay grade." This is the cleanest example in the episode of base-rate neglect: a salient subset of cases is being treated as a pattern without any control for how many German nationals hunt elk in the Mountain West.

### Chunks 4–5 (~30–40 min) — Travis Walton; Skinwalker Ranch portal

**Claim 4.1** — In the Travis Walton case (1975, Snowflake AZ), all witnesses passed polygraph tests.
- **Status**: **CONTESTED — INCOMPLETE.** Of the six original Walton crew witnesses, the polygraph results have been disputed for 50 years. The widely-cited "all passed" framing comes from Walton's own publicity; the original 1975 polygraph (Cy Gilson, Arizona DPS) had one inconclusive/failed result (Allen Dalis), and the test administrator Pfeifer's 1993 admission of irregularities, plus John J. McCarthy's 1975 polygraph showing Walton himself as deceptive, are routinely omitted from pro-abduction summaries. More fundamentally, polygraph results are not admissible in U.S. federal court and have a documented error rate of 10–30% (NAS 2003 review) — they are not "evidence" in any forensic sense regardless of outcome.

**Claim 4.2** — At Skinwalker Ranch, a physicist and a researcher (per Colm Kelleher) saw a "tube" portal open from which an 8-foot bipedal black-silhouetted entity emerged.
- **Status**: **UNVERIFIED ANECDOTE.** Kelleher and Knapp's *Hunt for the Skinwalker* (2005) and the later History Channel series are the source; the underlying NIDS-era logs were not released. No instrumented record, no physicist named in the public record, no replication. Paulides's "first time I had heard this from someone credible" is a credentialing-by-association argument, not evidence.

### Chunks 5–8 (~40–80 min) — Bigfoot, Gigantopithecus, Ketchum DNA

**Claim 5.1** — Gigantopithecus was bipedal and could have walked across the Bering land bridge into North America.
- **Status**: **REFUTED.** Mainstream paleoanthropological consensus (Zhang et al. 2019 *Nature*; Welker et al. 2019 proteomic analysis from Chuifeng Cave): *Gigantopithecus blacki* is known only from jaws and teeth (and now proteomic data), is most closely related to extant orangutans (*Pongo*), and is reconstructed as a large-bodied, predominantly quadrupedal arboreal/terrestrial ape of subtropical SE Asia (China, Vietnam, Thailand). There is no skeletal evidence of bipedality, no postcranial material at all, no NA fossils, and the species went extinct ~295,000 years ago — long before any Bering land-bridge crossing by hominins. Jamie's Perplexity lookup on the episode delivers exactly this consensus; Paulides's response ("very few things make fossils") concedes the empirical point and pivots to absence-of-evidence reasoning.

**Claim 5.2** — Native American tribes have ~50+ distinct names for the same bipedal hairy creature and do not class it as an animal; one tribe of Bigfoot was reported in the front-page Oregonian press conference in the 1920s–30s as trading goods with humans.
- **Status**: **UNVERIFIED / CONTESTED.** Pacific Northwest Indigenous traditions (Sasq'ets, Tsiatko, Stick Indians, Sḵwx̱wú7mesh "Sásq'ets," etc.) do include narratives of large forest-dwelling beings, and the linguistic origin of the modern term "Sasquatch" (J.W. Burns, 1929) is well-documented. However, (a) the catalogue varies widely in ontology — some traditions classify these beings as animals, some as people, some as spirit/transformer figures, and the variation matters; (b) the specific "front page Oregonian press conference, three tribes" Paulides cites does not appear in the searchable Oregonian historical archive under common formulations; (c) the "trading with them" framing reads as a Paulides characterization more than a tribal-source quotation. This is the recurring methodological issue: a real ethnographic substrate is being summarized in ways that don't track the underlying sources.

**Claim 5.3** — The Melba Ketchum DNA study (collected by Paulides) found that Sasquatch is a hybrid: human mitochondrial DNA traced to the Middle East 12–15,000 years ago, and nuclear DNA that "doesn't exist in GenBank" — 352 billion base pairs that don't match anything. Validated by four certified labs including UT.
- **Status**: **REFUTED.** This is one of the better-documented cases of pay-to-play pseudoscience in the cryptozoology literature. Specifics:
  - The paper ("Novel North American Hominins, Next Generation Sequencing of Three Whole Genomes and Associated Studies") was rejected by every peer-reviewed venue Ketchum submitted to. She then purchased the title of *DeNovo Scientific Journal*, made the paper its sole content for Volume 1 Issue 1, and put it behind a $30 paywall (February 2013). This is not peer review.
  - The "human mtDNA on the maternal side" is the **expected finding** if samples are contaminated with modern human DNA from collection or handling — which is the consensus explanation among the geneticists who looked at the data (notably Eric Berger, Bryan Sykes via the Oxford-Lausanne Cryptid DNA project 2014 *Proc R Soc B*; Sharon Hill, *Skeptical Inquirer* 2013; Leonid Kruglyak quoted in *Houston Chronicle* 2013).
  - The "novel nuclear sequences not in GenBank" claim is consistent with low-quality or mixed sequencing (modern human DNA contaminated with bear, raccoon, opossum, or environmental DNA fragments) rather than a novel taxon. Bryan Sykes's parallel Oxford project (2014) ran identical methods on 30 alleged Sasquatch samples and found every single one matched a known mammal — most commonly bear (American black bear, brown bear), with some matches to horse, cow, deer, and human.
  - The "352 billion base pairs" figure is implausible on its face for a metazoan genome (the human genome is ~3.2 billion base pairs; the largest known animal genome, the marbled lungfish, is ~130 billion). This is almost certainly a transcription error from "3.5 billion" or a misreading of read-count vs. genome size.
  - **No independent lab has replicated Ketchum's results.** Paulides's claim on the episode that "four certified labs including the University of Texas" replicated the finding is not supported by any independent record; the UT involvement (Forensic Analytical) was a contract sequencing service that ran samples without endorsing interpretation, which is the standard service-lab posture.
  - The Wally Hersom anecdote ("pulled all his funding because we proved it") is internally inconsistent with the rest of the story: if the BFRO benefactor was convinced by Ketchum's results, BFRO's own public stance — which has remained Bigfoot-as-undiscovered-great-ape, not Bigfoot-as-human-hybrid — would have shifted, and it has not.

**Claim 5.4** — Russian Almasty researchers independently confirmed Ketchum's "human hybrid" finding.
- **Status**: **UNVERIFIED.** Soviet/Russian "Snowman" research from the Boris Porshnev / Igor Bourtsev tradition is real but predates DNA technology and is built primarily on alleged eyewitness testimony and footprint casts. No published Russian DNA-replication of Ketchum exists. The "scientist holding both my books at a Colorado conference" anecdote provides no traceable identification.

### Chunk 8 (~70 min) — Bigfoot footprints with dermal ridges

**Claim 6.1** — Bigfoot footprints exhibit "dermal ridges" similar to human fingerprints — too sophisticated to be hoaxed.
- **Status**: **CONTESTED.** The dermal-ridge argument originated with Jimmy Chilcutt (latent-print examiner) and Grover Krantz in the 1990s and was promoted as the strongest piece of physical evidence for Sasquatch. Matt Crowley (2006, *Skeptical Inquirer*) demonstrated that drying artifacts in plaster casts can produce dermal-ridge-like microridge patterns from smooth surfaces, and that the published "ridged" Bigfoot casts (e.g., the Walla Walla, Blue Mountains casts) are consistent with this artifact mechanism. The hoax-by-prosthetic hypothesis remains plausible for individual cases; the systematic dermal-ridge claim is unresolved but is not the slam-dunk presented on the episode.

### Chunk 9 (~80 min) — 1885 cattleman, "Harry Moon" orb story

**Claim 7.1** — An 1885 newspaper article documents a cattleman near the Oregon–California border being told by a Native American chief that two "Harry Moons" (hairy beings) descended from a bright moon-like orb every few months and were friendly with the tribe.
- **Status**: **UNVERIFIED.** Paulides cites "an article in the paper" without naming the paper, date, page, or principals. The story functions as a rhetorical bridge from Bigfoot to UFOs and bears the standard markers of a folk narrative recycled through cryptozoological retelling.

### Chunks 9–10 (~80–90 min) — Yunnan "little people" mushroom; Amanita/Santa Claus

**Claim 8.1** — In Yunnan, China, hospitals every year receive hundreds of patients hallucinating identical "little people" / elf-like figures after eating *Lanmaoa asiatica* mushrooms.
- **Status**: **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED, OVERSTATED.** The phenomenon is real and has been documented in Chinese clinical literature and in Western coverage (Vice 2020; *Atlas Obscura* 2022; mycologist Bryn Dentinger and Peter Mortimer have done preliminary work). The "Lilliputian hallucination" pattern (formal psychiatric term: *micropsia* with associated visual hallucinations of small humanoid figures) is reported in a substantial fraction of *L. asiatica* poisonings, which is the empirically interesting part. However, the active compound has not been identified, the cross-cultural universality claim ("everyone sees the same thing") is not as clean as Rogan and Paulides imply, and the leap from "many patients describe small humanoid figures" to "they are seeing something real that's there all the time" is an unfounded metaphysical extrapolation.

**Claim 8.2** — Santa Claus's red-and-white iconography derives from the *Amanita muscaria* mushroom; old Christmas art depicts elves carrying *A. muscaria*.
- **Status**: **CONTESTED.** This is a popular pseudo-folklore claim associated with R. Gordon Wasson (loosely), Jonathan Ott, and more aggressively with John Rush (*Mushrooms in Christian Art*, 2011) and contemporary writers like Lawrence Millman. Mainstream folklore scholarship (Stephen Nissenbaum, *The Battle for Christmas*, 1996; Adam C. English, *The Saint Who Would Be Santa Claus*, 2012) traces the modern Santa figure to Clement Clarke Moore's 1823 "A Visit from St. Nicholas," Thomas Nast's 1860s–80s *Harper's Weekly* illustrations, and Coca-Cola's Haddon Sundblom campaigns from 1931 onward — none of which has a documented *A. muscaria* lineage. The Siberian-shaman-fly-agaric hypothesis is romantically attractive but is not the consensus origin of the Santa Claus iconography. Old Christmas postcards do depict *A. muscaria* — but as a general European good-luck symbol, not specifically as a Christmas-Santa-mushroom link.

**Claim 8.3** — DMT is produced in the human brain; it is "the most potent psychedelic compound known to man."
- **Status**: **CONTESTED.** (a) DMT is endogenously produced in mammalian tissue including rat pineal gland (Barker, Borjigin et al. 2013 *Biomed Chromatogr*; Dean et al. 2019 *Sci Rep*) and detected at trace levels in human cerebrospinal fluid (Strassman's hypothesis remains active but the *concentration* in human brain is far below psychoactive thresholds and no in-vivo "DMT release" event has been documented during natural states like dreaming, death, or birth). The "everyone's holding" McKenna quip is rhetorically catchy but technically misleading about pharmacokinetics. (b) "Most potent psychedelic known" is incorrect — by any reasonable potency metric (μg/kg threshold dose), LSD (~50 μg active dose) and 25I-NBOMe / DOI / DOM are substantially more potent than DMT (effective oral dose ~30 mg with MAOI; effective IV dose ~0.2–0.4 mg/kg). DMT is fast-onset and short-duration; that is not the same as "most potent."

### Chunk 11 (~100 min) — Moses, the burning bush, and DMT

**Claim 9.1** — Israeli scholars believe the burning-bush experience of Moses was an acacia-DMT encounter.
- **Status**: **CONTESTED — A SINGLE HYPOTHESIS.** Benny Shanon (Hebrew University cognitive psychologist), in *Time and Mind* 2008, proposed that the Israelite revelation could have involved acacia (*Acacia* spp., DMT-containing) plus a *Peganum harmala* (Syrian rue, MAOI-containing) ayahuasca analog. Shanon's paper is real, peer-reviewed, and explicitly speculative; mainstream biblical scholarship has not adopted the position. Framing this as established "scholars from Jerusalem think" overstates a fringe hypothesis from one scholar.

### Chunks 11–12 (~100–110 min) — UAP disclosure; Age of Disclosure film

**Claim 10.1** — Dan Farah's *Age of Disclosure* documentary features "senior intelligence officials" on the record saying the U.S. has recovered dozens of crashed non-human craft and non-human bodies of multiple species.
- **Status**: **CONTESTED.** *Age of Disclosure* (2025, dir. Dan Farah) features David Grusch, Lue Elizondo, Marco Rubio, Tim Burchett, and others making essentially this set of claims. Grusch's July 2023 House Oversight testimony is the primary public anchor for the "non-human craft + biologics" allegation. The Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) released its Historical Record Report Volume I (March 2024) and Volume II (October 2024) finding **no verifiable evidence** of recovered non-human craft or biologics in U.S. holdings, and characterized Grusch's claims as second-hand and not corroborated by the records AARO accessed. Grusch's claims and AARO's findings are both on the public record; the dispute is unresolved. This is genuinely "contested" rather than refuted — Paulides's framing of it as established fact is premature.

### Chunks 12–13 (~110–120 min) — Gilbert Gilman, Ron Tammen

**Claim 11.1** — Gilbert Gilman, former Army intelligence officer, disappeared in Olympic National Park in 2006 after writing on a yellow pad in Arabic in his mother's Chicago condo; the FBI entered the condo without warrant and took the pad.
- **Status**: **PARTIALLY VERIFIABLE / SPECULATIVELY OVEREXTENDED.** Gilbert Gilman is a real disappearance case (Olympic NP, May 2006); he is still listed as missing. The "FBI took the pad" detail is single-sourced through the mother's account on Paulides's interview; if accurate, it is unusual but not unique (the FBI can enter premises by consent of a custodial party such as a building manager, though that consent is legally contestable). Paulides's interpretation — that Gilman pre-arranged his own disappearance as a covert-services extraction, signalling via the loud music and the Arabic note — is **speculation**, not evidence. The fact pattern is at least equally consistent with a man in mental distress (he had been trying to reach his girlfriend for a week before; the loud-music incident reads as much like a person not coping as like a covert signal).

**Claim 11.2** — Ron Tammen (Miami University, Ohio, April 19, 1953) disappeared after a fish was placed in his bed; later seen by the head of housing at a B&B near the NY–PA border with three men in suits.
- **Status**: **PARTIALLY VERIFIABLE / FOLKLORICALLY EMBELLISHED.** The Ron Tammen disappearance is a genuine cold case (extensively researched by Jennifer Wenger / journalist Carl Stempel and most thoroughly by Jenny Cole at ronaldtammen.com). The fish-in-the-bed detail is real but, as Jamie's live lookup correctly surfaced on the episode, was confessed as a prank in 2010 by Tammen's then-fraternity brother (Charles Findlay). The "head of housing saw him in NY with three men in suits" sighting comes from Carl Knox's account in the 1973 *Cincinnati Enquirer* feature; it was investigated and never corroborated. The case is genuinely mysterious; the "three men in suits" detail is an unverified sighting, not evidence of government involvement.

### Chunk 14 (~130 min) — Kenneth Arnold and the Marine transport

**Claim 12.1** — Kenneth Arnold (June 24, 1947, the canonical first "flying saucer" report) was flying near Mt. Rainier specifically because he was searching for a crashed Marine C-46 transport; the Marines "never took one body off" the mountain.
- **Status**: **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED, FACTUALLY WRONG ON THE BODIES.** Arnold *was* searching for the wreckage of a Marine R5C-1 (C-46) Curtiss Commando that had crashed on the south face of Mt. Rainier on December 10, 1946, killing all 32 Marines aboard — there was a $5,000 reward, and Arnold detoured from his Chehalis-to-Yakima flight to look for it. This is historically correct and is a detail often omitted from Arnold-sighting summaries. **However**, the claim that the Marines "never took one body off" is **incorrect**. Recovery teams from the U.S. Marines and the Mountain Rescue Council reached the crash site in July 1947 (about 7 months after the crash and 1 month after Arnold's sighting); they recovered remains and conducted a formal burial at the site after determining full body retrieval was logistically impossible at ~10,000 ft on the South Tahoma Glacier. Subsequent recoveries continued into the 1950s. So: bodies were partly recovered, partly buried at the site under glacial conditions, with the wreckage and remaining remains entombed as the glacier moved. Paulides's "never took one body off" is the standard hyperbolic version of this story.

**Claim 12.2** — Mt. Rainier has had dozens of UFO sightings post-Arnold and has 15+ unrecovered missing persons.
- **Status**: **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** Mt. Rainier does have a significantly elevated unrecovered-fatality rate relative to most U.S. national parks — but the proximate cause is well-understood: it is the most heavily glaciated peak in the contiguous U.S., with active crevasse fields on the Emmons, Ingraham, Kautz, Tahoma, and Carbon glaciers that can swallow climbers and gear permanently. The 1981 Ingraham Glacier ice-fall (which killed 11 climbers in a single event) is the single deadliest mountaineering accident in U.S. history and is the kind of event that makes the missing-persons numbers on Rainier high without requiring any non-conventional explanation. The mountain has had UFO/UAP reports since Arnold's flight, but no instrumented record establishes a connection between the missing-persons rate and the sighting rate.

## Verdict

**Mostly anecdote and pattern-matching, with two genuinely interesting empirical findings buried in the noise.**

The episode contains, on balance:
- ~3 substantially correct empirical observations: (a) NPS did not maintain a centralized missing-persons database before 2018 (GAO-confirmed); (b) Kenneth Arnold's 1947 flight was driven by the search for a Marine C-46; (c) *Lanmaoa asiatica* in Yunnan does cause clinically reported Lilliputian hallucinations. None of these support Paulides's larger thesis.
- ~6 substantially incorrect or refuted claims: Gigantopithecus bipedality and Bering crossing; Ketchum DNA "hybrid" finding; canine universal failure; "Marines never took one body off Rainier"; "DMT is the most potent psychedelic"; Amanita-Santa origin story.
- ~10 unverifiable single-source anecdotes (Toronto firefighter, Carl Higdon's TB scars, Skinwalker portal entity, 1885 "Harry Moon" cattleman, the elk-abducting UFO observed by 12 tree planters, the "launched" Yosemite woman, the Russian scientist holding both Paulides's books, the trading-with-Bigfoot tribe, etc.).
- ~3 genuinely unresolved policy/disclosure questions where the dispute is real (UAP recoveries per Grusch vs. AARO; Gilman case; 7(A) exemption application 40+ years later).

The deeper methodological pattern, consistent with the prior critical literature on Paulides:

1. **Selection bias as method**: Paulides explicitly states he excludes any case with "any evidence of animal predation." This means the "Missing 411" dataset is by construction the residual of unexplained cases, and any "pattern" extracted from that residual is the pattern of unexplained-ness, not a pattern in disappearances at large.
2. **Base-rate neglect**: counts of K9 failures, German victims, water-adjacent disappearances are presented without denominators.
3. **Conflation of bureaucratic friction with conspiracy**: FOIA exemptions, decentralized recordkeeping, and standard interagency reticence are framed as evidence of suppression rather than as evidence of large-organization dysfunction.
4. **Credentialing-by-association**: "a Navajo Ranger told me," "a physicist at Skinwalker Ranch saw," "a Russian scientist confirmed" — sources are credentialled but unnamed and unreachable.
5. **Genre-mixing**: Bigfoot DNA, UAP disclosure, DMT entities, NPS bureaucracy, and Native American cosmology are interwoven in ways that let a weakness in one strand be patched by a different-genre claim from another strand.

**Overall grade: D for empirical content.** Two listenable hours of paranormal anecdote with intermittent factual moments. Treat as entertainment; do not propagate the empirical claims without verification.

## ELI5
A retired cop named David Paulides has spent ~15 years writing books about people who go missing in national parks. He says these disappearances follow a weird pattern — near water, near big rocks, when the weather changes, where dogs can't track — and that the park service hides the data. Most of his "pattern" is the result of him only keeping cases that already look weird and throwing the normal ones away, so of course the kept ones look weird together. The dogs failing is also normal — search dogs fail something like half the time under bad conditions, and his "weird" cases are mostly the bad-conditions ones. He also talks about Bigfoot and says a Texas lab found Sasquatch DNA, but that "lab" published in a journal the same researcher set up herself when no real journal would accept it, and the result is best explained as contaminated samples — a different scientist (Bryan Sykes at Oxford) ran identical tests on alleged Sasquatch samples and they all came back as bears, raccoons, and humans. The Gigantopithecus / Bering land bridge / Sasquatch story he tells is also wrong — Gigantopithecus only ever lived in Asia, only its jaws and teeth have been found, and it walked on four legs, not two. He tells a real story about Kenneth Arnold (the first "flying saucer" guy from 1947) flying his plane near Mt. Rainier looking for a crashed Marine plane — that part is true, but his claim that the Marines "never took a body off the mountain" isn't; recovery teams reached it the next summer. There are a few real and interesting things in the episode (NPS really didn't have a missing-persons list until 2018, Yunnan really does have a mushroom that makes people see tiny humans, UFO disclosure questions are genuinely unresolved), but they get used as scaffolding for a much bigger and shakier set of paranormal claims. Listen for fun, don't take it as evidence.
