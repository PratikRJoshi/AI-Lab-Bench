## Analysis

### Medical: SORT Analysis

**Identified claims (extracted from transcript + on-screen overlay):**
- **C1** — Personal aetiology: recent grief and "excess stress" triggered her current Hashimoto's flare ("lost grief, excess stress, that'll do it").
- **C2** — Strategy framing: in a Hashimoto's flare, the priority intervention is to "get inflammation off of my body stat," and this is achieved by an unprocessed-majority diet tracked on calories + protein + fiber.
- **C3** — Low-glycemic, fiber-rich produce (and "low GI" mangoes as a controlled sweet treat) are helpful in a Hashimoto's flare context.
- **C4** — "Homemade adrenal cocktails" (made with the carrots she is buying — typically orange juice + coconut water + sea salt ± cream of tartar in the functional-nutrition canon) are part of flare management.
- **C5** — "The best most collagen rich bone broth" — implied therapeutic / anti-inflammatory benefit relevant to Hashimoto's flare resolution.
- **C6** — (Truncated, inferred) Adding a cinnamon-class spice to a dish "tops with [helps] blood sugar" — implicit blood-sugar / insulin-resistance management benefit.

#### Pass 1 — Four lenses

- **Safety**:
  - C1–C3 are safety-neutral on their face — adequate protein, fiber, and predominantly unprocessed foods are mainstream-aligned nutritional advice and do not displace levothyroxine or endocrine monitoring (neither of which is mentioned).
  - **C4 (adrenal cocktail)** carries low but non-zero safety risk: the *Endocrine Society* and a peer-reviewed *BMC Endocrine Disorders* (2016) systematic review classify "adrenal fatigue" as a non-existent / pseudo-endocrine disorder; Cleveland Clinic and WebMD endocrinology coverage note that the added potassium load from coconut water + orange juice can be harmful in people with chronic kidney disease and that the cocktail does not replace evaluation for true adrenal insufficiency (Addison's). The bigger harm is **diagnostic delay** — viewers with real symptoms (fatigue, low BP, hyperpigmentation) may self-treat with a juice instead of seeking ACTH-stimulation testing.
  - **C5 (bone broth)** is generally safe; the practical safety issue is opportunity cost / cost-of-care if it is framed as a meaningful inflammation lever.
  - Whole-clip safety issue: no mention of when a Hashimoto's flare warrants thyroid-function re-testing or a clinician visit. The post implicitly normalises self-management of an autoimmune flare without endocrine follow-up.
- **Outcomes (POEM vs DOE)**:
  - No numerical outcomes are named — no antibody titres (TPO / TG), no TSH/T4 values, no levothyroxine dose change, no symptom score. The implied POEM is "flare resolution"; the implied DOEs are "inflammation reduction" and "blood-sugar control."
  - Of the relevant published literature: the dietary-intervention evidence in HT is **mostly DOE** (anti-TPO antibody titre changes); the *patient-oriented* outcome "shorter flare" has no controlled-trial endpoint at all.
  - Per SORT rule: outcomes lens alone caps the strongest evidence here at Grade B.
- **Risk of bias**: n=1 personal testimonial from a creator whose business depends on the credibility of nutrition-led Hashimoto's management. No control, no blinding, no objective verification (pre-/post-flare antibody panel, TSH, hsCRP) is shared on-camera. Survivorship bias: she has already self-described remission, which selects her into the population of "people for whom this *appears* to have worked."
- **Total evidence**:
  - **For C1 (stress as flare trigger)**: real but limited signal. A 2019 RCT (Markomanolaki et al., *Hormone and Metabolic Research*) of an 8-week stress-management intervention in women with Hashimoto's reported reduced anti-TG antibodies and lower stress/anxiety/depression scores — small, single-site, but supports the biological plausibility. A 2024 *Biomedicines* review of environmental factors in AITD lists stress among plausible triggers while noting the direct flare evidence is sparse. A 2023 *BMC Endocrine Disorders* meta-analysis showed a strong stress→onset effect in Graves' disease (not Hashimoto's) but found no significant effect on drug-therapy outcomes. Net: B-grade plausibility, no large or definitive RCT.
  - **For C2 / C3 (unprocessed, fiber-rich, calorie- and protein-tracked diet for HT)**: a 2023 *Nutrients* systematic review (Osowiecka & Myszkowska-Ryciak) of nutritional interventions in HT (9 studies) found that energy-deficit diets and elimination of selected ingredients (gluten, lactose, goitrogens) improved anti-TPO, TSH, and fT4 in some trials; results are heterogeneous and limited by small sample sizes. There is no head-to-head trial of "macro tracking + unprocessed foods" specifically. The framing is consistent with mainstream nutrition advice and at minimum supports a Grade B for surrogate (DOE) endpoints in HT.
  - **For C4 (adrenal cocktail)**: best evidence is overtly negative. Wierman et al. (Endocrine Society) and Cadegiani & Kater (2016 *BMC Endocrine Disorders*) systematic review explicitly conclude that "adrenal fatigue" is not substantiated; Cleveland Clinic endocrinology explicitly states no evidence that cocktails reduce cortisol, stress, or restore adrenal function beyond ordinary hydration and nutrient intake. Grade C / pseudo-endocrine framing.
  - **For C5 (bone-broth collagen → reduced inflammation in HT)**: no human RCT in any thyroid condition. Closest evidence is a 2021 murine ulcerative-colitis model (Mar-Solís et al., *Medicina*) showing bone broth reduced IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α and increased IL-10 in mice. The 2022 *International Journal of Rheumatic Diseases* systematic review on collagen supplementation in RA and OA found 19 trials of variable quality, high adverse-event rates in some studies, and was unable to conclude whether collagen is beneficial. No HT data. Grade C for the human-inflammation framing.
  - **For C6 (cinnamon / blood sugar)**: cinnamon's effect on fasting glucose and HOMA-IR has been studied in T2DM with mixed meta-analytic results (mostly Grade B DOE evidence in T2DM, not in HT). Marginal/uncertain relevance to a Hashimoto's flare.

#### Pass 2 — SORT grade

- **C1 (stress / grief precipitated this flare)** — **Grade B.** One small RCT + observational signal support a stress→AITD biology; no trial measures "flare resolution" as a POEM.
- **C2 (unprocessed, calorie + protein + fiber tracked diet → reduced inflammation in flare)** — **Grade B for DOEs (antibody titres, TSH)**; the *unprocessed/fiber-rich* part of the message is also aligned with non-disease-specific mainstream guidance and is the safest, most defensible piece of the vlog.
- **C3 (low-GI + fiber produce; mango as "low glycemic" sweet treat)** — **Grade B** for the broad concept (fiber + lower-GI choices in insulin-resistance-prone HT population, which she correctly notes elsewhere is common). Minor factual quibble: mangoes are **moderate-GI** (~51 unripe, up to ~60 ripe), not "low-glycemic" — it is a borderline single-serving choice, not a contraindicated one.
- **C4 (homemade adrenal cocktails)** — **Grade C.** Predicated on a pseudo-endocrine concept ("adrenal fatigue") that endocrinology societies and a peer-reviewed systematic review have classified as not real. The drink itself is mostly harmless juice + salt, but the framing is not evidence-based.
- **C5 ("most collagen rich bone broth" implied therapeutic / anti-inflammatory benefit)** — **Grade C** for HT-relevant inflammation; only murine evidence and inconclusive arthritis-collagen evidence. Bone broth as food is fine; as a flare-resolution lever it is mechanistic hand-waving.
- **C6 (cinnamon / blood sugar, inferred)** — **Grade B (in T2DM, by extrapolation)**, unverified relevance to a Hashimoto's flare.

**Overall reel grade: B–/C+.** The defensible scaffolding (stress trigger, unprocessed-food diet with adequate protein and fiber, glycemic awareness) is Grade B. The decorative flourishes that mark this as functional-medicine content — adrenal cocktails and collagen-rich bone broth as flare interventions — are Grade C. Because the vlog spends roughly equal screen-time on both, and because the on-screen overlay frames the *entire* shopping trip as therapy for an autoimmune flare, the post averages to a low-B / high-C overall.

## Verdict
This is one of the more reasonable Hashimoto's-content reels in this batch. April Rodriguez's underlying message — *stress can precipitate a flare; in response, I'm shopping for adequate protein, plenty of fiber, low-glycemic produce, and mostly unprocessed food* — maps cleanly onto what published nutrition and stress-management literature can broadly support at SORT Grade B (with the caveat that the outcomes measured are usually antibody titres / TSH, not "flare resolution"). The presentation is calm, food-positive, and free of supplement upsell within the clip itself, and she correctly characterises her response as personal flare management rather than a cure. What pulls the post down from a clean Grade B is the embedded functional-medicine vocabulary: **"adrenal cocktails"** is rooted in the "adrenal fatigue" concept that endocrinology societies and a 2016 peer-reviewed systematic review have explicitly classified as pseudo-endocrine, and **"most collagen rich bone broth"** as an anti-inflammatory lever for autoimmune flare has only murine-model evidence behind it. Neither item is unsafe in isolation, but together they advertise an evidence framework that conflicts with thyroidology. Add in the unstated commercial context — her main business is 1:1 paid Hashimoto's coaching, repeatedly described on her own site and in founder interviews — and the appropriate reading is "credentialed nutrition coach sharing personal flare management with a recognisable functional-medicine accent, on a content channel that ultimately funnels to a paid program." Worth sharing with a friend with Hashimoto's only if you flag the adrenal-cocktail framing as not evidence-based and pair it with the reminder that flares warrant a check-in with their endocrinologist, not just a Whole Foods cart.

If you're flaring, eat the way she's eating if it appeals to you, but also call your endocrinologist — vlogs don't replace bloodwork.
