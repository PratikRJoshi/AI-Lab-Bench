## Analysis

### SORT Analysis

#### Pass 1 — Four Lenses

**Safety**
No safety concerns relevant to the walking recommendation. Walking is among the lowest-risk physical activities, with no meaningful absolute risk of harm. No harms are omitted because none exist for the claim being made.

**Outcomes**
The core claims are patient-oriented (POEMs) — all-cause mortality is the hardest, most clinically meaningful endpoint. This is a strength. However, the numbers are presented as relative risk without baseline context:
- "8% lower risk" and "60% lower risk" are relative risk reductions. The absolute risk reduction depends on baseline mortality rate, age, and health status. A 60% relative risk reduction in a young, healthy population may represent a small absolute mortality benefit over any given timeframe, while the same figure in an older high-risk population is far more clinically significant.
- The carousel does not state what comparison group these figures are measured against (i.e., 2,800 steps vs. what baseline? vs. sedentary individuals? vs. 1,000 steps/day?).

**Risk of Bias**
The specific numbers closely match findings from a 2022 Lancet Public Health meta-analysis (Paluch et al.) of 15 international cohorts covering ~47,000 adults. This is a large, rigorous, multi-cohort analysis — the gold standard of observational evidence. The risk of bias is low for an observational study of this design. The major irreducible limitation of all step-count studies is confounding: healthier, more mobile people walk more, making it difficult to fully isolate walking itself as the causal mechanism.

**Total Evidence**
The step-count mortality relationship is one of the most consistently replicated findings in physical activity epidemiology:
- Paluch et al. (2022, Lancet Public Health): ~8,000-8,800 steps/day associated with ~60% lower all-cause mortality vs. ~2,000 steps/day (HR 0.40).
- Tudor-Locke et al., multiple WHO physical activity guidelines, and a 2025 updated systematic review all confirm a dose-response relationship starting around 2,600-3,000 steps.
- The "8% lower at 2,800 steps" figure is directionally consistent with the dose-response curve in the Paluch data, though this specific number doesn't appear verbatim in the landmark paper — it likely reflects a specific curve point in the data.
- Consistent with WHO (2020) guidelines recommending 150-300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, which ~8,000 daily steps roughly maps to.

#### Pass 2 — SORT Grade: B

**Grade B** — The underlying evidence (Paluch et al. 2022, Lancet) is from a well-conducted systematic review and meta-analysis of observational cohorts. However:
1. All step-count mortality studies are observational — not RCTs. Causality cannot be definitively established from this design alone.
2. The relative risk framing without absolute risk comparator is a presentational limitation.
3. SORT Grade A requires RCT-level patient-oriented evidence; observational cohort studies, however large, max out at Grade B.

The core message (walk more, especially if currently sedentary, and 8,000-9,000 steps/day is the evidence-backed target) is accurate and consistent with current scientific consensus.

## Verdict

The walking statistics in this carousel are accurate and well-sourced from one of the strongest datasets in physical activity epidemiology (Paluch et al., Lancet, 2022, 15 cohorts, ~47,000 adults). The key nuance missing is that "60% lower risk" is a relative risk reduction compared to highly sedentary individuals (~2,000 steps/day) — the absolute benefit varies significantly by age and baseline health. That said, the directional message is solid: even modest increases in daily walking (reaching 7,000-9,000 steps/day) provide substantial mortality benefit, especially for sedentary people. The remaining 8 images in the carousel are self-promotional and make no health claims. SORT Grade B.
