## Analysis
### Medical: SORT Analysis

**Decomposed claim set (extracted from thumbnail overlay + visual context):**
1. Women with Hashimoto's commonly have "hormonal imbalances," "puffiness / water retention," and "chronic inflammation."
2. These three problems "can be helped by supporting detox pathways."
3. (Implied by visual) Rebounding on a mini-trampoline is one way to support those pathways (lymphatic drainage / "detox").

#### Pass 1 — Four Lenses

**Safety**
The post itself is low-immediate-risk (an exercise modality on a small trampoline), but it is unsafe-by-omission in three ways relevant to Hashimoto's:
- It frames symptom relief around "detox pathways" rather than the established first-line intervention: levothyroxine titrated to TSH/FT4, monitored by labs. A viewer with under-treated hypothyroidism who pursues "detox" instead of (or in addition to) endocrinology follow-up can develop worsening fatigue, dyslipidemia, and — at the severe end — myxedema.
- "Puffiness/water retention" in Hashimoto's can be true myxedematous edema, which is a sign that thyroid replacement is inadequate, not a sign that lymphatic flow is the problem. Treating it with rebounding instead of dose adjustment delays appropriate care.
- Mini-trampoline injuries (ankle sprains, falls, occasionally serious fractures or head injuries) are not negligible; the post shows unsupervised jumping with no safety framing. No NNT, NNH, contraindications, or harms are mentioned.

**Outcomes (POEM vs DOE)**
The outcomes asserted — feeling less puffy, less inflamed, more hormonally balanced — are vague subjective surrogates rather than validated POEMs (no mention of TSH/TPO normalization, no quality-of-life instrument, no antibody trajectory, no symptom-score scale). "Supporting detox pathways" is not itself a measurable outcome; "detox" has no operationalized definition in clinical medicine for healthy livers and kidneys. The strongest POEM evidence relevant to the population (women with Hashimoto's) belongs to: adequate levothyroxine replacement (mortality and morbidity benefit for overt hypothyroidism), selenium supplementation in selenium-deficient regions (modest reduction in anti-TPO antibodies — DOE), and general physical activity (cardiometabolic POEMs that are not Hashimoto's-specific). None of these is what the post is implicitly selling.

**Risk of Bias**
The claim is sourced from a paid coach's personal social-media testimony, with no study citation, no protocol, no measurement, and a direct financial incentive to position "detox / root cause" framing as the answer. This is the bottom tier of the evidence pyramid (anecdotal expert opinion). The underlying "rebounding drains the lymph and detoxes the body" claim, when traced upstream, originates in wellness writing rather than peer-reviewed lymphology; the only direct trial (Berger et al., 2018, lymphedema pilot) enrolled 7 women and is not Hashimoto's-related.

**Total Evidence**
Mainstream sources contradict or simply do not support the implied mechanism:
- The McGill Office for Science and Society explicitly characterizes "rebounding to detox" as a hazardous solution to a non-existent problem, noting the absence of evidence that trampolining improves lymphatic flow in healthy people.
- ATA (American Thyroid Association) and Endocrine Society guidance on Hashimoto's centers on levothyroxine, TSH monitoring, and (where deficient) selenium; "detox pathways" are not in the guideline vocabulary.
- Systematic reviews of dietary/supplement interventions in Hashimoto's (Pyzik et al. 2023; Osowiecka & Myszkowska-Ryciak 2023; Piticchio et al. 2023) find only limited, very-low-certainty effects of specific named interventions (gluten-free diet, selenium, vitamin D) on antibody titers and no consistent POEM benefit; none of these reviews evaluate "detox pathway support" or rebounding because those are not coherent clinical interventions.

**Overall grade: C.**

| Sub-claim | Best-evidence grade |

The descriptive premise (Hashimoto's women experience hormonal shifts, edema, inflammation) is medically accurate, but the therapeutic premise (the lever to pull is "detox pathways," shown via rebounding) is mechanistic hand-waving with no patient-oriented trial backing. Per the SORT rubric, mechanistic reasoning without clinical outcome data and a vague intervention not defined in clinical medicine = Grade C.

## Verdict
The opening observation is real: women with Hashimoto's frequently do experience hormonal symptoms, fluid retention, and inflammation. But the post then routes those very real symptoms toward a fuzzy non-medical construct — "supporting detox pathways" — visually anchored to rebounding on a mini-trampoline. Neither the construct nor the implied intervention has Grade A or B evidence in autoimmune thyroiditis: "detox pathways" is not a defined therapeutic target in any endocrinology guideline, and the rebounding-as-lymphatic-drainage literature consists essentially of one 7-person pilot in lymphedema plus wellness-industry marketing. Meanwhile, the actually evidence-based moves for these symptoms (correctly dosed levothyroxine titrated to TSH, screening/treating selenium deficiency where present, general exercise, gluten-free trial in selected patients, sleep, weight management) are not mentioned. Combined with a direct commercial pipeline into paid Hashimoto's coaching and a non-licensed nutrition credential, the trustworthiness ceiling for this specific post is low. The post earns Grade C: directionally compassionate, mechanistically unfounded.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
She's right that women with Hashimoto's often feel puffy, hormonal, and inflamed — that part is real. But "support your detox pathways by bouncing on a trampoline" is wellness marketing, not medicine. Your liver and kidneys already detox you for free, and the actual fixes for Hashimoto's symptoms are boring: the right dose of thyroid meds, regular bloodwork, sleep, normal exercise, sometimes selenium if you're deficient. Bouncing on a mini-trampoline is fine cardio if you enjoy it — just don't expect it to fix your thyroid antibodies, and don't skip the endocrinologist for it.
