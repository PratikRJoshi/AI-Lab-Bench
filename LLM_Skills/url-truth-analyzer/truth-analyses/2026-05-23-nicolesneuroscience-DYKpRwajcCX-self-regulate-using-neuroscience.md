## Analysis

### Claim Validation

**Claim 1 — Stressed → "Breathe using physiological sigh."**
*Supported.* The physiological sigh (a double inhale through the nose followed by an extended exhale through the mouth) has direct human RCT evidence. Balban et al., *Cell Reports Medicine* 2023 randomised 114 adults to 5 min/day of cyclic sighing, box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation, or mindfulness meditation for one month; cyclic sighing produced the largest improvements in positive affect and the largest reduction in respiratory rate. Mechanism: the second inhale re-inflates collapsed alveoli, the prolonged exhale offloads CO₂ and increases parasympathetic vagal tone. *Caveat:* effects are modest and acute; this is a regulation tool, not a substitute for treatment of clinical anxiety.

**Claim 2 — Anxious → "Walk for amygdala deactivation."**
*Partially supported.* The cleanest direct evidence is Sudimac, Sale & Kühn, *Mol Psychiatry* 2022: 60-minute walks in nature (vs. urban environments) reduced amygdala activity during a stress task on fMRI; urban walks did not. So the strong form ("walking deactivates the amygdala") really requires a *nature* walk; generic walking has weaker neuroimaging support, though acute aerobic exercise broadly reduces state anxiety (Stonerock et al., 2015 meta-analysis) via mechanisms including BDNF release, HPA-axis modulation, and rhythmic locomotor distraction. The slide collapses these distinctions but is directionally correct.

**Claim 3 — Sad → "Move before you trust every thought."**
*Supported as a behavioural heuristic; loose as neuroscience.* Two ideas are blended: (a) cognitive defusion / "don't believe every thought" from third-wave CBT and ACT (Hayes et al.) — well-supported as a clinical technique; and (b) behavioural activation for low mood, where action precedes mood improvement rather than waiting for motivation (Dimidjian et al., *J Consult Clin Psychol* 2006; Ekers et al. 2014 meta-analysis showing BA non-inferior to CBT for depression). The slide's framing is a reasonable lay paraphrase. *Caveat:* this is general advice, not a treatment plan for clinical depression, and "every thought" is rhetorical hyperbole.

**Claim 4 — Impulsive/Angry → "Look outside, opening your gaze can help you think clearly."**
*Contested — weakest claim in the carousel.* This is the "panoramic vision" / optic-flow hack popularised by Andrew Huberman, who argues that broadening from focal to panoramic gaze deactivates the sympathetic "tunnel-vision" stress response and recruits parasympathetic tone. The mechanistic story (focal/foveal viewing pairs with mydriasis and arousal; panoramic viewing pairs with calming, akin to optic-flow during walking) is plausible and references real visual-system anatomy, but **direct peer-reviewed human evidence that panoramic gaze acutely reduces impulsivity or anger is sparse**. Most of the citation trail is Huberman-podcast and self-help repetition, with primary studies focused on optic-flow during locomotion (Bakker et al.) or visual attention more generally. Treat as a low-cost folk technique with weak evidentiary backing, not a settled finding.

**Claim 5 — Low motivation → "Start small. Momentum boosts dopamine."**
*Partially supported.* The behavioural part — start small to overcome activation energy — is identical to behavioural activation and habit-stacking (Fogg, *Tiny Habits*; Lally et al. on habit formation). The "momentum boosts dopamine" mechanism is a folk shorthand for two real findings: (a) dopaminergic neurons fire to *predicted* and *anticipatory* cues, not just rewards (Schultz, *Annu Rev Neurosci* 2007), so initiating a goal-directed action generates anticipatory dopamine; and (b) progress feedback ("near-miss" / completion cues) sustains striatal activation. *Caveat:* "boosts dopamine" is doing a lot of work — the slide collapses anticipatory dopamine signalling, reward prediction error, and motivational vigour into a single phrase. Useful heuristic, imprecise mechanism.

**Claim 6 — Low self-worth/Insecure → "Write down your strengths. Engages logical thinking which overrides emotional brain."**
*Supported in spirit, oversimplified mechanism.* Two real literatures back this:
- *Self-affirmation theory* (Steele 1988; Cohen & Sherman, *Annu Rev Psychol* 2014): brief writing exercises affirming personal values/strengths buffer threat responses, lower cortisol reactivity, and reduce defensive processing.
- *Cognitive reappraisal* / top-down emotion regulation (Ochsner, Bunge, Gross & Gabrieli, *J Cogn Neurosci* 2002 and follow-ups): reappraising negative stimuli increases dorsolateral and ventrolateral PFC activity and decreases amygdala activity.
*Caveat:* the "logical brain overrides emotional brain" framing is the discredited triune-brain trope (MacLean) — the PFC↔amygdala interaction is bidirectional and far more distributed than "thinking part overrides feeling part." The recommended action is sound; the mechanistic gloss is pop-neuro shorthand.

## Verdict
Mostly trustworthy as a starter menu of self-regulation moves, with one weak entry. Five of the six prescriptions (physiological sigh, walking — especially in nature, behavioural activation for low mood, starting small, and self-affirmation/reappraisal for low self-worth) are anchored in real peer-reviewed evidence and would be uncontroversial advice from a clinical psychologist or mainstream neuroscientist. The "look outside / open your gaze" rule for impulsivity is the outlier — plausible mechanism, but the direct human evidence is thin and the citation trail leans on Huberman-podcast repetition rather than primary studies. The slide language ("amygdala deactivation," "momentum boosts dopamine," "logical brain overrides emotional brain") consistently flattens nuance into one-liners — fine as mnemonic, misleading if read as precision neuroscience. Vignola's credentials are real, no fact-check flags exist, and she has commercial reasons to keep slides crisp; treat the carousel as a starting menu, not a protocol.

## ELI5 — Friend to Friend
Mostly legit. The breathing one (the double-sniff sigh) actually has a clean Stanford study behind it, and "go for a walk, especially in nature" beats the amygdala in real fMRI. The one I'd put a small asterisk on is "look outside to calm down when you're impulsive" — sounds plausible, costs nothing, but the actual research backing is thin (it's mostly a Huberman-podcast claim). The "logical brain overrides emotional brain" framing is a vibe more than science, but the underlying move (writing down your strengths when you feel bad) is genuinely supported. Take it as a friendly fridge-magnet checklist, not a treatment plan.
