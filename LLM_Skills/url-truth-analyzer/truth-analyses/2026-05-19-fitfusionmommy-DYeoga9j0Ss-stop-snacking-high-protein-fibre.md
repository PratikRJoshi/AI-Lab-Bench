## Analysis
### Medical: SORT Analysis

**Extracted claims**
1. Eating high-protein, high-fibre meals reduced the creator's between-meal snacking (anecdotal POEM).
2. The featured Indian/vegetarian-friendly meals (rajma, paneer dishes, tofu masala, moth dal, palak chowli, quinoa moong, vermicelli with paneer, etc.) are appropriate "high protein + fibre" options.
3. Tofu in masala curries is a near-essential protein vehicle for vegetarians (CTA slide).

**Pass 1 — Four lenses**

1. **Safety lens** — Risk surface is low: these are whole-food Indian meals with no supplement, no fasting protocol, no extreme restriction. No absolute-vs-relative-risk concerns because no risk-reduction is quantified. Unspoken caveats the post omits: (a) chronic kidney disease patients should not chase high-protein patterns without clinician input; (b) high-fibre intake should ramp up gradually to avoid GI distress, especially with legumes (rajma, moth dal, chowli, mung); (c) soy/tofu is contraindicated in confirmed soy allergy and is debated (though not contraindicated by current evidence) for those on levothyroxine taken close to soy meals; (d) "low-fat paneer" and "protein bread" are commercial products whose macros vary by brand.

2. **Outcomes lens** — The headline outcome — reduced snacking / lower inter-meal hunger — is patient-oriented (a POEM: subjective satiety, eating behavior, often a proxy for energy balance). Protein has the highest satiety index of the three macronutrients in head-to-head feeding studies (Holt et al., 1995; Weigle et al., 2005). Dietary fibre, particularly viscous and fermentable fibres, increases gastric distension, slows gastric emptying, and stimulates GLP-1 and PYY release, which independently suppress appetite (Slavin & Green, 2007; Clark & Slavin, 2013). Both mechanisms have RCT-level POEM support.

3. **Risk of bias lens** — The post itself is a single-creator anecdote with no study cited (low-tier evidence on its face). But the underlying mechanism is supported by multiple independent randomized crossover feeding studies and at least two well-designed meta-analyses; the bias risk is in the *framing* ("these specific meals fixed my snacking") rather than the macro-level claim. The post does not disclose total daily energy, portion sizes, or training context — common omissions that turn a defensible claim into an unfalsifiable testimonial.

4. **Total evidence lens** — Consistent with mainstream guidance: ISSN, Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, and dietary guidelines worldwide endorse higher-protein eating (≈1.2–2.0 g/kg/day in active adults; ≈25–30 g per meal to maximize muscle-protein-synthesis stimulus) and fibre intake at ≥25 g/day for women / ≥38 g/day for men. The post's macro framing is congruent with these positions; it doesn't extend beyond them into anything novel or contested.

**Pass 2 — SORT Grade**

- Claim 1 (high-protein + high-fibre meals reduce snacking): **Grade B** — supported by multiple RCTs and at least one well-conducted meta-analysis with patient-oriented outcomes (hunger ratings, ad-libitum intake), but with heterogeneity in study design (acute appetite studies vs. multi-week weight-loss trials), variable effect sizes, and recurring limitations around isocaloric control.
- Claim 2 (the featured Indian meals are reasonable high-protein/high-fibre options): **Grade B** — mechanistically and compositionally accurate (legumes deliver ~7–9 g protein and ~6–8 g fibre per 100 g cooked; paneer ~18 g protein/100 g; tofu ~10–17 g protein/100 g depending on style), but no per-meal gram totals are given in the post.
- Claim 3 (tofu masala curries are essential for vegetarian protein): **Grade C** — overstated. Vegetarians can hit protein targets with paneer, dal, rajma, chana, Greek yogurt, eggs (lacto-ovo), or whey/pea isolate; tofu is one option, not the only one.

**Overall post grade: B** — the central message and the meal exemplars are evidence-aligned, but the prescriptive tofu-CTA and absent portion/energy context downgrade it from A.

## Evidence / Validation Links

1. Weigle DS, et al. "A high-protein diet induces sustained reductions in appetite, ad libitum caloric intake, and body weight despite compensatory changes in diurnal plasma leptin and ghrelin concentrations." *Am J Clin Nutr*, 2005;82(1):41–48. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/ — Foundational POEM evidence that protein increase suppresses appetite and reduces ad-libitum intake.

2. Leidy HJ, et al. "The role of protein in weight loss and maintenance." *Am J Clin Nutr*, 2015;101(6):1320S–1329S. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/ — Review of higher-protein eating and satiety/weight outcomes; supports the "protein reduces snacking/hunger" mechanism with caveats on study heterogeneity.

3. Clark MJ, Slavin JL. "The effect of fiber on satiety and food intake: a systematic review." *J Am Coll Nutr*, 2013;32(3):200–211. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23885994/ — Mixed but generally positive effects of fibre on satiety; viscous and fermentable fibres most consistently effective.

4. Reynolds A, et al. "Carbohydrate quality and human health: a series of systematic reviews and meta-analyses." *The Lancet*, 2019;393(10170):434–445. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/ — Large meta-analysis establishing the ~25–30 g/day fibre target with POEM-level outcomes (mortality, T2D, CVD, CRC).

5. Morton RW, et al. "A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults." *Br J Sports Med*, 2018;52(6):376–384. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/ — Grade-A support for ~1.6–2.2 g/kg/day protein targets that the carousel's meal exemplars are sized to hit.

## Verdict
The central claim — that high-protein, high-fibre meals reduce constant snacking — is genuinely supported by multiple RCTs and reviews showing protein and fibre each independently increase satiety and reduce ad-libitum intake via well-characterized hormonal and gastric mechanisms. The featured meals are compositionally consistent with that approach: legumes, paneer, tofu, yogurt, and lean prawn provide protein; the legumes, leafy greens, and whole grains supply fibre. Where the post overreaches is the closing CTA, which singles out tofu masala curries as a near-essential vegetarian fix without addressing soy considerations or naming the equally adequate alternatives the carousel itself displays (rajma, paneer, eggs, dal, yogurt). On balance: Grade B, share with caveats — useful inspiration for someone reorganizing meals around protein and fibre, not a precise prescription, and not a reason to mono-source tofu.
